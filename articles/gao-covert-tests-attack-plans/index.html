WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

In spring 2026, Government Accountability Office investigators logged in as ordinary users to ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Grok. All four produced information usable for planning attacks. Two platforms supplied plans to build and detonate improvised explosive devices for mass casualties; the other two supplied plans targeting the power grid, including drone strikes on substations and shooting at substations, aimed at causing economic damage.

Each platform produced its plan in under half a day, with specific targets, locations, timing and methods. The GAO verified feasibility through research, surveillance and expert interviews. All four platforms first triggered safeguards, refusing to answer, deleting content or suspending accounts, and investigators bypassed every one of them with common circumvention techniques.

The report is dated October 16 and was published on November 18. In the public version, the GAO deliberately withheld which platform matched which scenario, and did not disclose the prompts or circumvention techniques, to prevent imitation. Senator Maggie Hassan, who requested the audit and will chair the Senate Homeland Security Committee, plans a hearing on AI safety in January.

WHAT TO WATCHWhat the report leaves out is worth reading too

The GAO hid which platform matched which scenario, and the prompts and circumvention techniques were not disclosed. The omission is deliberate: publishing the details would hand imitators an instruction manual.

The conclusion's limits also matter. This was a point-in-time assessment, not a statistically significant sample. All four companies said they improved safeguards after the testing, and Anthropic added that it could not verify the GAO's findings. The report tested the spring versions; today's products may not look the same.

WHY IT MATTERSThe yardstick for evaluation needs changing

The report's impact is not that one company's safeguards were bypassed, but that all four fell to the same playbook. However high a lab benchmark score runs, it cannot answer a more practical question: what can an ordinary person extract from a product in half a day?

The hearing is set for January, and the agenda already extends beyond any single company's fixes. Whether regulators should set a single real-world testing standard for frontier-model safety assessments is the next round of argument.

THE SHIFTWhat Changed

FROM BENCHMARKS TO REAL USERS
From lab scores to what an ordinary person can extract
Benchmarks measure how a model performs under controlled conditions; covert testing measures what an ordinary user can obtain in half a day. The two yardsticks give different answers, and regulators' attention is shifting from the former to the latter.
FROM ONE FAILURE TO FOUR
From a single failure to a collective failure
One platform being bypassed could be blamed on its own safeguards; all four falling to the same playbook points to a problem with the industry's shared approach to safeguards.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISSafeguard failure is a systemic problem

Tokenta Signal's judgment: safeguard failure is a systemic problem, with all four platforms falling to the same playbook. Safety evaluation can no longer rest on lab scores alone; it must ask what an ordinary person can extract from a product in half a day. The report hides which platform matched which scenario, the gaps in safeguards are in plain view, and closing them will take more than stricter filters.

WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAINWhat Remains Uncertain

The report notes this was a point-in-time assessment, not a statistically significant sample, and its conclusions do not describe the versions on sale today. All four companies said they improved safeguards after the testing, and Anthropic said it could not verify the report's findings. The report makes no recommendations, because its subjects were third-party commercial products, not federal agencies. This does not constitute investment advice.
