$625 Million: Oura Buys an AI Doctor Company

Oura announced on November 10 it will acquire AI-native primary care company Counsel Health for $625 million in cash, expected to close before the end of 2026. Counsel's medical AI and licensed physicians will join the Oura app, and founder Muthu Alagappan becomes Oura's Chief Clinical AI Officer.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

The acquisition target is a service, not hardware. Counsel Health, founded in 2023, runs AI-native primary care: users first chat with a medical AI, then a licensed physician takes over diagnosis and prescriptions. About 70 employees, $36 million raised in total.

The price is 17 times the funding. Counsel closed a $25 million Series A last year, led by a16z and GV. Oura is paying $625 million in cash for the combination of medical AI and a physician network.

The two companies have worked together since May, with Counsel's service listed in the Oura app's Labs section. The acquisition brings the partnership in-house, and founder Muthu Alagappan becomes Oura's Chief Clinical AI Officer.

FROM TRACKING TO TREATINGFrom Recording Bodies to Being a Care Entry Point

Oura has more than 6 million members; its rings record sleep, heart rate, and temperature every day. With Counsel's physicians in, the data connects to diagnosis and prescriptions for the first time.

Oura CEO Tom Hale told Bloomberg in an interview that the acquisition connects wearables, clinical AI, and physician care. Only the three together make a complete medical entry point.

$533M QUARTERThe Money Comes From a Strong Quarter

Oura also released preliminary results: revenue of $533 million for the quarter ended September 30, up 153% year over year, with adjusted EBITDA of $87 million. Fiscal 2026 revenue is about $1.75 billion, up 92%.

The results were announced the same day as the acquisition, and the timing is no coincidence. Strong cash flow is the backing for a $625 million all-cash deal.

IPO SHELVED, DEAL DONEIPO Just Shelved, Deal Done First

Weeks earlier, Oura indefinitely postponed an IPO of up to $2.2 billion, citing market uncertainty. The listing was estimated at a valuation of about $15 billion.

Not listing does not mean not spending. The first big move after the IPO delay is an acquisition: Oura traded the wait for a listing window for a window to buy. The IPO can wait; the entry-point position cannot.

THE SHIFTWhat Changed

FROM LOGGING TO TREATING
From passive logging to active treatment
The ring used to record only sleep and heart rate; now the data connects to physicians, enabling consultations, diagnosis, and prescriptions. The wearable has gone from a health diary to a medical entry point.
FROM WAITING TO BUYING
From waiting to list to buying first
After the IPO delay, Oura did not sit and wait for a window; it used cash to buy the entry point directly. A listing is a way to raise money; an acquisition is a way to take position, and the two do not wait for each other.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISThe Band Has Finished Measuring the Body; Now It Sells Care

Tokenta Signal's judgment: the wearable war is changing tracks, from who measures most accurately to who can turn data into treatment. The $625 million buys not a clinic but the last mile from data to prescription. An IPO can wait; the entry-point position cannot.

WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAINWhat Remains Uncertain

The deal has not yet closed; completion is subject to customary conditions. Reports differ on timing: Reuters and The Verge say before the end of 2026, TechCrunch says before the end of 2027; this piece follows the company's announcement of before the end of 2026. Oura has not disclosed pricing or covered countries for physician consultations inside the app, nor how merged user biometric data will be handled. The ring itself is not a medical device and is not used for diagnosis or treatment. This does not constitute investment advice.
