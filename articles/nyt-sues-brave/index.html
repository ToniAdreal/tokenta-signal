The New York Times Sues Brave Over the 30 Billion Pages in Its Index

The New York Times sued browser company Brave on November 2, accusing it of scraping copyrighted content and distributing it to multiple AI companies. Three other newspaper publishers filed similar suits the same day; the complaint cites an index of more than 30 billion pages and the Paywall blocker feature.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

The New York Times sued Brave in Manhattan federal court on November 2, accusing the browser company of scraping its copyrighted content and distributing it to AI companies including Anthropic, Perplexity, Mistral, xAI and Cohere. The same day, the publishers of the New York Daily News, the Chicago Tribune and the Orlando Sentinel filed similar lawsuits.

At the core of the complaint is an index of more than 30 billion pages. The plaintiffs say Brave built the index with a crawler that publishers cannot identify or block, then charged AI companies for access to the real-time online content in it, used to train models or answer user questions.

The suit also targets the browser's Paywall blocker feature. The plaintiffs say the feature lets users bypass the paywalls of hundreds of publishers. The Times describes Brave in the complaint as a supplier, dealer and distributor of stolen copyright, and seeks an injunction and damages.

SUING THE SUPPLIERThe Defendant Is Not a Chatbot, but a Data Supplier

Most earlier copyright suits went straight at the model companies. This one is different: the defendant is the supplier in the middle layer. The plaintiffs' logic is blunt. The content the models consumed exists because someone first carried it out and sold it.

If that logic holds in court, every link of the AI data supply chain has to be re-costed. Scraping, indexing and reselling could each constitute infringement on its own. The middleman is no longer a safe zone.

THE UNBLOCKABLE CRAWLER30 Billion Pages, and the Question Is Whether the Crawler Can Be Blocked

The most damaging line in the complaint is that publishers cannot identify or block this crawler. robots.txt works only if the crawler chooses to follow the rules; a crawler that does not declare itself bypasses the entire voluntary mechanism.

That is also the contrast with Issue 109's report on the robots.txt opt-out signal. On one side, four large companies promised to honor a unified opt-out signal; on the other, plaintiffs accuse someone of not playing the game at all. Rules and rule-breaking are colliding in the same week.

PAYWALL IN THE DOCKThe Paywall Claim Drags the Browser Itself Into Court

Beyond index reselling, the complaint also accuses the Paywall blocker feature of helping users bypass paywalls. That part of the claim targets the browser product itself, not the data business.

Together, the two claims put Brave in a two-front fight. The data business model and the legality of a browser feature will be tested in the same lawsuit. The case could take years, and the product keeps running in the meantime.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISThe Copyright War Has Reached the Middle of the Supply Chain

Tokenta Signal's judgment: the next battlefield of AI copyright disputes is not the models, but the people who supply them. Once the legality of indexes and crawlers is ruled on, the entire data supply chain has to rewrite its rules.

WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAINWhat Remains Uncertain

The limits of this report: at this stage there are only the plaintiffs' claims. The court has made no findings, and Brave has not made a substantive response in public reporting. All descriptions of Brave's conduct in this report are allegations from the complaints, not established facts.
