FBI Warns ARTEX AI Attack Tool Is Powering Global Cyberattacks

The FBI warned on November 13, 2026 that threat actors have used ARTEX, an AI-powered penetration-testing framework, to automate cyberattacks worldwide since at least August 2026, with some intrusions reaching privileged access in under three hours. The advisory cites the October ConnectWise supply-chain breach, which exposed more than 2,250 Microsoft 365 tenants, and attacks on South Korean banks that exposed about 67,000 people's data.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

The FBI warned on November 13, 2026 that multiple threat actors have used ARTEX, an AI-powered penetration-testing framework, to automate cyberattacks against organizations worldwide since at least August 2026. In FLASH advisory FLASH-20261113-001, the bureau said the tool has targeted organizations across financial services, government, technology, defense, healthcare, semiconductor, energy, manufacturing, telecommunications and legal sectors.

ARTEX is an autonomous framework written in Go that uses AI agents to perform reconnaissance, discover vulnerabilities, exploit systems, escalate privileges and steal data with little human intervention. It has no model of its own; it plugs into external large language models including Anthropic Claude, OpenAI GPT, DeepSeek, GLM, Grok and Kimi, converting an attacker's goals into commands executed by specialized agents. The FBI said the framework lets actors with minimal technical expertise run complex, multi-target operations at once.

The advisory names the October attack on ConnectWise Cloud Backup as a leading example. Attackers compromised application identities used by the backup service, formerly known as SkyKick Cloud Backup, and used them to attempt authentication against more than 2,250 downstream Microsoft 365 tenants. Huntress identified at least 162 compromised tenants. Microsoft attributed the campaign to China-state-aligned actor Storm-0940, which used ARTEX to accelerate reconnaissance, password spraying and abuse of cloud application permissions; some intrusions advanced to credential changes and attempts to establish persistent access.

WHY IT MATTERSSpeed is the story, not a new vulnerability

"ARTEX significantly reduces the time required to chain attack techniques, with some intrusions progressing from initial targeting to privileged access in under three hours," the FBI wrote. The framework finds the systems organizations watch least: partner portals, employee applications, legacy services and third-party integrations. Automation generates large volumes of suspicious traffic that can trigger alerts, but the speed leaves defenders little time to respond before a breach lands.

The tool was released on GitHub in July 2026 by a developer known as Autumn-27 as a legitimate testing aid, and removed on October 8 after reports of criminal abuse. Removal did not stop distribution: the FBI says ARTEX remains available through community forks, archived copies, localized versions and a commercial edition called ARTEX PRO. Researchers had identified more than 1,600 publicly exposed ARTEX consoles by early November, with the true number of deployments believed to be significantly higher.

WHAT TO WATCHThree campaigns show the pattern

Beyond ConnectWise, the advisory cites attacks on South Korean financial institutions that exposed personal information belonging to about 67,000 people. A Chinese-speaking actor combined ARTEX with Claude Code agents to target Shinhan Bank, KB Kookmin Bank and Hana Bank; CrowdStrike found the attacker's exposed infrastructure containing ARTEX configuration files and Claude Code session histories. South Korean police arrested seven suspects linked to attacks on more than 90 organizations, in which ARTEX was reportedly used in most intrusions, stealing about 8.2 million data records.

CrowdStrike separately reported that Chinese cyberespionage group Vapor Panda used ARTEX in automated attacks against more than 200 organizations in 14 countries, compromising at least nine, with AI agents performing reconnaissance, exploiting vulnerabilities, deploying web shells, stealing credentials and trying alternative paths after failed attempts. The FBI's advice is defensive and concrete: secure internet-facing applications, restrict cloud-administration access, review application permissions and authentication logs, rate-limit sensitive APIs, and watch for bursts of automated requests, unexpected credential changes and large data transfers. Cybersecurity researcher MalwareMustDie released an open-source ARTEX detection-signature pack on November 13 to help defenders spot the activity.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISThe barrier to entry just fell

Tokenta Signal's judgment: ARTEX does not invent a new kind of attack; it industrializes the old ones. Reconnaissance, exploitation and privilege escalation were always automatable in pieces, and now a free framework chains them end to end under a natural-language goal, in the hands of actors with minimal skill. The ConnectWise case is the proof that matters: one compromised backup application became a doorway into thousands of tenants. The defense the FBI prescribes is unglamorous and specific, which is exactly why it is worth doing: lock down the forgotten portals, audit the third-party app permissions, and treat a burst of automated requests as an intrusion in progress, not noise.

WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAINWhat Remains Uncertain

The facts rest on the FBI's FLASH advisory FLASH-20261113-001, released November 13, 2026, as reported by BleepingComputer on November 15, 2026, read in full, and by Dataconomy on November 16, 2026, read in full. The ConnectWise tenant figures come from Microsoft's October investigation and Huntress's reporting as relayed by both outlets; Microsoft notes that an attempted sign-in to a tenant does not prove data theft, and that most of the 2,250 targeting attempts failed. The South Korean arrest and record-theft figures come from South Korean police reporting relayed by BleepingComputer. This does not constitute investment advice.
