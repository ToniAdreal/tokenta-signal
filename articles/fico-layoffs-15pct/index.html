FICO Cuts 15%: AI Is Just the Fig Leaf

The truth behind FICO's 15% layoffs is not in 'AI transformation'. It is in a document from Washington. The broken monopoly is why the stock halved; AI is just the respectable wording for the earnings call.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

On Tuesday, October 6, FICO announced layoffs of about 15%. The company declined to confirm the exact number; based on 3,811 employees at the end of September 2025, roughly 570 people are affected, and notifications began this week. The official rationale was a 'simplified operating structure' plus 'AI-driven product development', in its words: 'This simplified structure will allow us to operate and bring innovations to market faster and create more value for our customers.'

The financial cost: an expected pre-tax charge of about $27 million in Q4 FY2026, mostly severance, with the restructuring substantially complete by Q3 FY2027. But the market is not buying it: the stock slipped 0.1% pre-market the next day to $694.80 and is down roughly 58% this year. Barron's headline was blunt: 'FICO Cuts 15% of Its Staff. Why Investors Aren't Buying It.' The company earned $652 million in net income last year; a $27 million severance charge is a drop in the ocean.

THE FIG LEAFThe AI fig leaf: earnings-call rhetoric

Reuters characterised FICO as 'another US company cutting jobs as it shifts spending toward AI for corporate automation tasks' — a standard member of the 2026 AI layoff wave. But the rhetoric has a flaw: if this were truly AI efficiency, the stock should rise. Instead it fell another 0.1% pre-market the day after the announcement. Investors voted with their feet: they do not believe it.

'AI-driven restructuring' is becoming the most versatile sentence of the 2026 earnings season: it explains layoffs, hints at the future, and takes no responsibility for the past. But FICO's problem is not in the future. It is in the past — the past in which it made money lying down.

THE REAL BULLETThe real bullet: it came from Washington

The main cause of the share-price collapse was written last month: Bill Pulte, director of the Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA), directed Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac to allow all lenders to use rival VantageScore (developed jointly by Equifax, Experian and TransUnion) under a unified pricing schedule. That single document directly shook FICO's monopoly in mortgage credit scoring, and the stock suffered its worst single-day fall since 1989 on the news.

The logic of a monopoly business is simple: pricing power is profit. A unified price schedule confiscates FICO's sharpest blade. The money saved by cutting 15% of staff and the money lost to a loosening monopoly are not in the same order of magnitude.

THE MATH570 people, $27 million — no fix for the fundamentals

Do the maths: last year's net income was $652 million, so a $27 million severance charge is under 4% of it; roughly 570 people (media estimate, not officially confirmed) against a base of 3,811 saves mostly salaries. The restructuring completes by Q3 FY2027 — but VantageScore's competition will not wait until 2027.

That is why investors are not buying it: layoffs solve a cost problem, and FICO has a revenue problem. When a monopoly breaks, the correct move is to rebuild the moat, not to simplify the structure. Using 'AI transformation' as a fig leaf covers the earnings call. It does not cover the share price.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISA fig leaf cannot cover the bleeding

The most fashionable layoff rhetoric of 2026 is 'AI-driven'; the most honest reason is 'business is bad'. FICO has both: the former in its announcement, the latter in its share price. When a company starts explaining present-tense problems in the future tense, it means the present tense can no longer be explained.

WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAINWhat Remains Uncertain

A $27 million severance charge is a rounding error against $652 million in net income, and 570 jobs are a rounding error against the loosening of a monopoly. The question FICO really has to answer is not 'how much can AI save', but 'what are you worth without the monopoly' — and layoffs cannot answer that question.

EVIDENCEEvidence

A 15% cut of roughly 570 jobs, a $27 million severance charge and a stock down 58% this year: the real bleeding point is the FHFA opening the door to VantageScore and breaking the mortgage-scoring monopoly.
