WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

The Financial Times reported on November 2, 2026, citing five people familiar with the matter, that Nvidia has told cloud computing and data center partners worldwide to ensure at least 51% of their services are sold to non-Chinese companies, effectively capping Chinese customers at 49% of business under future contracts.

The guidance was delivered verbally through Nvidia's sales staff and regional teams over recent months rather than in writing, and it applies only to newly signed contracts: existing computing-rental agreements are not affected retroactively. No such quota existed previously, and the rule covers partners worldwide, not only facilities in Asia.

Nvidia neither confirmed nor denied the report to Agence France-Presse, saying in a statement that data centers and clouds built on American chips provide the foundation US model and application makers need to compete globally. China's foreign ministry spokeswoman Mao Ning said she was not aware of the report and urged a fair, transparent and non-discriminatory business environment.

A counterweight arrived the same day: The Information, citing three sources, reported the guidance is not currently mandatory or strictly enforced. A Singapore-based provider quoted in the Financial Times coverage said finding enough non-Chinese clients outside the United States is difficult and warned strict enforcement would hurt its business.

The reported quota tightens earlier supply controls. Nvidia had already cut the number of Asian customers approved to buy its AI processors by more than half after creating a whitelist of companies that passed stricter compliance reviews, and US export controls have restricted sales of its most advanced AI chips to China since 2022.

WHY IT MATTERSA verbal quota is an enforcement question, not a settled rule

The form of the guidance matters as much as the number. A verbal instruction with no published effective date, no stated metric, and no audit mechanism leaves partners guessing whether 51% means revenue, compute hours, or customer count, and whether Chinese-owned subsidiaries abroad or resellers count as Chinese.

The Information's counter-report sharpens the uncertainty: guidance that is not mandatory today can still shape behavior, because partners dependent on Nvidia supply have reason to comply pre-emptively. The test is not the headline threshold but whether any partner is actually refused capacity or told to rebalance a contract. Until then, the 49% cap is best read as reported intent with contested force, not as a verified operating rule.

WHY IT MATTERSThe rental route is the real target

The quota's logic runs through rented compute, not chip shipments. Chinese firms including Alibaba and ByteDance have rented Nvidia-powered capacity in data centers in Singapore and Malaysia owned by non-Chinese companies, according to TheStreet's reporting, and a customer-mix rule reaches that route where export controls on hardware alone cannot.

That is also why the pain falls unevenly. Providers in Singapore and Malaysia must now find non-Chinese demand to keep growing, or scale back. If enforcement stays soft, the rule is mostly a signal to Washington; if it hardens, it redraws who can rent frontier compute in Asia, and at what price.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISThe number is reported; the enforcement is the story

Tokenta Signal's judgment: the 51% figure is less important than its status. A verbal, unconfirmed, possibly unenforced quota still moves behavior, because no partner dependent on Nvidia supply wants to be the test case. Watch for the first concrete enforcement, a refused contract or a forced rebalance, before treating the cap as a rule rather than a signal.

WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAINWhat Remains Uncertain

This item reports a press report, not a confirmed Nvidia policy. The Financial Times account rests on five unnamed people familiar with the matter; Nvidia has neither confirmed nor denied the 51% figure to Agence France-Presse, and Reuters carried the report without independently verifying it. The Information, citing three sources, reports the guidance is not currently mandatory or strictly enforced. No effective date, enforcement mechanism, or measurement basis for the 51% threshold (revenue, compute hours, or customer count) has been published.
