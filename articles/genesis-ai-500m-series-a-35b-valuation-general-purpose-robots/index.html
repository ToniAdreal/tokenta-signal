Genesis AI raised $500 million in Series A funding at a $3.5 billion post-money valuation on November 17, 2026, co-led by Premji Invest, Khosla Ventures and Eclipse, with NVIDIA, AMD Ventures and Eric Schmidt among the participants. The Paris and San Carlos company builds its full robotics stack in-house, GENE, a dexterous hand and data glove, Genesis World simulation and its flagship robot Eno, and will use the money to deploy Eno at scale in manufacturing, logistics and laboratories.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

Genesis AI, a Paris and San Carlos robotics company building general-purpose robots, raised $500 million in Series A funding at a $3.5 billion post-money valuation on November 17, 2026. The round was co-led by Premji Invest, Khosla Ventures and Eclipse, with Durable Capital Partners, T. Rowe Price, Bpifrance, HSG, NVIDIA, AMD Ventures, Zoom, Samsung Next and Ericsson taking part, alongside former Google chief executive Eric Schmidt, Zoom chief executive Eric Yuan, former Yahoo chief executive Jerry Yang, French entrepreneur Xavier Niel and former Tesla chief financial officer Zach Kirkhorn. Reuters called it one of the biggest funding rounds in Europe's robotics sector.

The raise comes about 16 months after Genesis emerged from stealth in July 2025 with $105 million in seed funding. The company builds its entire stack in-house: GENE, its robotics foundation model; a dexterous hand paired with a data-collection glove that maps human hand movements one-to-one onto the robot; Genesis World, a simulation platform for training and testing skills; and Eno, its flagship general-purpose robot, unveiled on June 16, 2026. Eno is designed for human capability rather than human appearance, and the company says it is now ramping up production.

The money will fund expanded data acquisition and large-scale training, work on human-level physical manipulation across environments, deployment of Eno at scale with customers, and hiring of AI researchers, robotics experts and engineers. Eno will first go to work in manufacturing, logistics and laboratories, then expand into hospitality, healthcare, consumer and outdoor applications, with a wider customer rollout expected in 2027. Genesis says its blog post sets out a longer arc: build the robots, put them to work in factories and warehouses, expand them as they grow more capable and affordable, and eventually have robots build more robots.

The team behind the raise carries weight in both AI research and capital. Co-founder and chief executive Zhou Xian leads a company co-founded by former Mistral AI researcher Theophile Gervet. Premji Invest partner Vedant Agrawal said what stood out was watching Genesis robots complete long, multi-step tasks on their own at the same speed a person would, with smooth and natural movement. Khosla Ventures founder Vinod Khosla said the full-stack approach gives the company a real opportunity to solve the challenges that have held back general-purpose robotics for years.

WHY IT MATTERSThe dexterity bet is now a $3.5 billion bet

Genesis is not selling a demo. It is selling a robot that does long, multi-step work at human speed, and the investors who watched it do so wrote the largest robotics cheque Europe has seen. A $500 million Series A at a $3.5 billion valuation, 16 months after a $105 million seed, is the market saying the bottleneck in general-purpose robotics is no longer the idea but the execution: data, hands, simulation and deployment, all built by one company in one closed loop.

The funding also marks a shift in who backs physical AI. NVIDIA and AMD Ventures are in the round alongside Khosla and Schmidt, which puts chipmakers on the cap table of a company whose robots will need their silicon at scale. The bet is that the winning general-purpose robot company looks less like a model lab and more like a vertically integrated manufacturer, and that the first to deploy reliably in factories wins the data flywheel that makes the next generation better.

WHAT TO WATCHDeployment is the test now

Watch whether Eno survives contact with real factory floors. The company says production is ramping and a wider customer rollout is expected in 2027, but no customer has been named, and the proof of a general-purpose robot is months of autonomous operation across whole workflows, not a filmed task. The first named deployments, and whether they expand, will say more than the valuation does.

Also watch the hand. The one-to-one mapping between a human hand, a data glove and the robotic hand is the core of the training loop: people doing their regular work in gloves generate the data that teaches the robot. If that loop works at scale, Genesis gains a data advantage rivals cannot buy. If dexterity stalls at demo tasks, the $3.5 billion valuation, the largest in European robotics, will look early. Either way, the round sets the pace for the field: Humanoid in Britain and Neura Robotics in Germany are now chasing the same industrial customers with the same pitch.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISEurope's biggest robotics cheque bets the bottleneck is execution, not ideas

Tokenta Signal's judgment: A $500 million Series A at a $3.5 billion valuation, 16 months after stealth, is the market deciding that general-purpose robotics is now an execution race, and that the winner builds everything itself. Genesis owns the model, the hand, the glove, the simulation and the robot, and its investors watched the system complete long tasks at human speed before writing the cheque. The valuation is justified only if Eno holds up in real factories for months, not minutes, and the first named customer deployments in 2027 are the test that matters. If they land, the closed loop of human data, simulation and deployment becomes a compounding advantage no rival can copy quickly. If they slip, this is the most expensive robotics demo Europe has funded.
