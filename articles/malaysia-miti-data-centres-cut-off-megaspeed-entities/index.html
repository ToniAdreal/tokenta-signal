Malaysia Tells Data Centres to Cut Off Megaspeed-Linked Firms

Malaysia's Ministry of Investment, Trade and Industry said on November 24, 2026 that it will advise the country's data centre operators not to host, lease space to, or provide services to the seven Megaspeed-linked entities the US Commerce Department added to its Entity List on November 23. Two of the seven are listed under Malaysia: Speedmatrix Sdn Bhd and MSFusion Sdn Bhd.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

Malaysia's Ministry of Investment, Trade and Industry (MITI) said on Tuesday that it will advise the country's data centre operators not to host, lease space to, or provide services to the seven entities the US Commerce Department added to its Entity List on November 23, 2026. The Malaysian Investment Development Authority (MIDA) will issue the notice to all operators in the country. MITI said Malaysia would not allow itself to be used as a channel to circumvent export controls, and that action would be taken against parties found to have broken the law.

Of the seven entities, two are listed under Malaysia: Speedmatrix Sdn Bhd, a wholly owned subsidiary of Singapore-incorporated Megaspeed International Pte Ltd, and MSFusion Sdn Bhd. MITI also clarified that PT Megaspeed International is not a Malaysian entity: based on MIDA's records, it is neither a data centre operator nor a tenant in Malaysia. The other listed entities are Megaspeed International Pte Ltd, Apex Enterprise Solutions, Swiftdata Pte Ltd, Megaspeed (Thailand) Co Ltd, and PT Megaspeed International itself.

The advisory is not a ban and names no deadline. MITI announced no enforcement action against the two Malaysian entities, and gave no details of MSFusion's business or its links to the other companies. MSFusion has denied any equity relationship with Megaspeed or its subsidiaries, and said Alice Huang never held any position at the company. Megaspeed chairman and chief executive James Tan Yong Pong said the company takes US export control requirements seriously and has engaged a US law firm to review its GPU inventory and compliance.

WHY IT MATTERSA third government just moved on Megaspeed — this time as a host country, not an investigator.

Washington blacklisted the firms and Singapore opened its own probe; Malaysia is now the first country to act on the ground where the hardware actually sits. Speedmatrix was a tenant at Malaysian data centres, and Malaysia's data centre boom — 55 of 117 approved projects already operational — is exactly the infrastructure the alleged diversion route ran through. The advisory tells operators that hosting a blacklisted tenant is now a compliance risk, not just a reputational one.

The move also closes a gap in the enforcement chain. The Entity List blocks US-origin goods from reaching the seven firms, but it does not by itself evict them from foreign data centres. Malaysia's notice does that work locally, and it signals to every other Southeast Asian host that the same question is coming. The diversion story is becoming a map of jurisdictions, and Malaysia just put its name on it.

THE WATCHPOINTAdvice, not enforcement — the gap between the words and the outcome.

MITI's language is careful: operators will be advised, not ordered, and no enforcement action was announced against Speedmatrix or MSFusion. The ministry also pushed back on the framing, insisting PT Megaspeed International is not a Malaysian entity at all. That leaves the practical question open: whether existing hosting contracts are wound down, and on what timetable.

The next markers are concrete. Watch for MIDA's notice going out and any operator confirming it has ended a tenancy; for the outcome of MITI's customs investigation into whether chips brought in through Speedmatrix were re-exported; and for MSFusion's denial to be tested against the company records showing overlapping directors and shareholding links with the Megaspeed group.

THE SHIFTWhat Changed

FROM BLACKLIST TO LANDLORD
From a US blacklist to a host-country compliance action
Malaysia's advisory turns the Entity List from a US export restriction into a local operating question for the data centres that actually host the hardware. The blacklist blocked goods; the advisory pressures tenancies — and it is the first such move by a Southeast Asian host country.
FROM ONE FIRM TO A ROUTE
From one firm's diversion to a regional route under scrutiny
With Singapore investigating, Malaysia advising operators, and the US blacklisting across three countries, the Megaspeed case is now a map of jurisdictions rather than a single company's conduct. The open question is whether the other host countries follow Malaysia's lead.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISThe blacklist now has a landlord: Malaysia just told its data centres to shut the door.

Tokenta Signal's judgment: the Entity List was Washington's move and Singapore's probe was the second act; Malaysia's advisory is the first move that touches the physical infrastructure. An advisory is weaker than an order, and MITI announced no enforcement — but it puts every Malaysian operator on notice that a blacklisted tenant is now a liability, and it puts the rest of Southeast Asia on the clock. Watch for the notice itself and for any operator that ends a tenancy: that, not the statement, is the moment the route starts to close.

WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAINWhat Remains Uncertain

The facts are as stated by Malaysia's Ministry of Investment, Trade and Industry on November 24, 2026, and reported by BusinessToday, The Business Times and The Edge the same day. The US Entity List action was covered in Flash 500 and Singapore's parallel investigation in Flash 508. The allegations against Megaspeed and Alice Huang remain allegations: the US Justice Department is investigating, and no charges have been announced. MSFusion denies any equity link to Megaspeed.
