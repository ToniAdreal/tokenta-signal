OpenAI Launches OpenWorks to Fund Local AI Experts for Small Businesses

On November 23, 2026, OpenAI launched OpenWorks, a program that funds local AI experts — OpenWorks Partners — to give US small businesses free, hands-on AI help. Partners earn a monthly stipend of up to $8,000, spend days each month with local businesses and host a monthly community workshop. Participating businesses get one-on-one support and 3 months of ChatGPT Business at no cost. The pilot is live in 20 US cities and open only to independently owned US small businesses.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

On November 23, 2026, OpenAI launched OpenWorks, a program that funds local AI experts — OpenWorks Partners — to give US small businesses free, hands-on help with AI. Partners dedicate days each month to working directly with local businesses and host a monthly community workshop. In return they receive tools, resources and workshop support plus a monthly stipend of up to $8,000. Participating businesses get one-on-one support and 3 months of ChatGPT Business at no cost.

The pilot is live in 20 US cities, including Chicago, Kansas City, Fargo, New York, Los Angeles, Seattle, Austin, Houston and Atlanta. It is open only to independently owned US small businesses, including restaurants, retailers, service firms and manufacturers. Software and SaaS companies, venture-backed businesses and private-equity-owned businesses are not eligible.

OpenAI is recruiting its first OpenWorks Partners now: technically skilled individuals and small teams committed to helping local businesses, with software and AI consultancies encouraged to apply alongside their existing client work. The official page already shows partners at work. In Fargo, partner Jake Ottiger helped gift shop Tiny Things build a ChatGPT inventory dashboard the owners estimate would have cost about $50,000 to commission; in Charleston, partner Jim Reilly helped microgreens grower Ghost Grow 843 lift its restaurant-outreach response rate from 20% to 75% and gain 10 new customers in four months.

WHY IT MATTERSDistribution, not a model, is the announcement.

OpenWorks adds no new model and no new feature. What it adds is funded human distribution: local experts who close the last-mile adoption gap for owners who would never buy a consultancy or read an API doc.

The $8,000 monthly stipend is the mechanism that makes that distribution possible. It turns AI-savvy locals into a paid field force, and it prices OpenAI's bet that in-person help converts small businesses faster than self-serve software ever could.

WHAT CHANGEDFrom self-serve tools to funded local help.

Small businesses previously got ChatGPT access and training resources to use on their own. OpenWorks puts a funded person in the room: a partner who works on the owner's chosen project, builds a tool the team can keep using, and teaches the staff to run it independently.

The offer to businesses is also different in kind. It is not a free trial of software but free labor plus 3 months of ChatGPT Business, with the partner's time paid for by OpenAI rather than by the business.

WHAT IS NEXTWatch who signs up, and whether 20 cities becomes nationwide.

The test is supply, not demand. OpenAI needs enough skilled partners in each pilot city to make the promise real, and the $8,000 stipend is the number that decides whether good operators apply.

If the pilot fills its cities and the case studies hold up, the question becomes scale: whether OpenWorks expands beyond its first 20 cities and whether the model survives contact with businesses whose problems are messier than an inventory dashboard.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISA Hiring Program Disguised as a Launch.

Tokenta Signal's judgment: OpenWorks is OpenAI paying for distribution. The product is not new software but funded people — local experts who turn small-business owners into ChatGPT users one shop at a time. The $8,000 stipend and the 20-city pilot are the real numbers; the Fargo $50,000 figure is one owner's estimate, not a measured saving, and should be read as a story rather than a result. The test now is whether enough skilled partners sign up, and whether the pilot grows past its first 20 cities.
