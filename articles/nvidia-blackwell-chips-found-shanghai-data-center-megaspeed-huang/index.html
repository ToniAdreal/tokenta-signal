Nvidia Blackwell Servers Surface at Shanghai Site Tied to Megaspeed's Alice Huang

Chinese regulatory loan filings reviewed by Bloomberg show Nvidia Blackwell hardware, a GB300 NVL72 rack and a GB200 server, at the Yangtze River Delta Project data center in Shanghai, a site linked to Alice Huang, founder of the blacklisted Singapore cloud firm Megaspeed. The filings are the first public evidence that restricted Blackwell chips reached the site; Bloomberg reported no evidence Megaspeed itself diverted the hardware, and the route remains unknown.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

Nvidia's most advanced AI server hardware has turned up at a data center in Shanghai tied to Alice Huang, the founder of Singapore-based cloud firm Megaspeed, Bloomberg reported on November 27, 2026, citing loan documents filed with Chinese regulators.

Shanghai Shuoyao offered a GB300 NVL72 rack, a system built on 72 Blackwell Ultra chips, as collateral for a 96 million yuan ($14 million) loan from Shanghai Innovation Bank, giving the rack's serial number in documents filed in November. A second company, Shanghai Kezhan, described a GB200 server at the same site in paperwork for a 90 million yuan loan from a finance-leasing firm wholly owned by a Shanghai government agency.

Blackwell chips cannot be shipped to China without a US license. The filings are the first public evidence that such processors reached the Yangtze River Delta Project, a site local media have described as planned to house as many as 800,000 servers. The same filings also list Hopper-generation hardware at the site, including 32 H200 servers and Super Micro and Dell servers used to secure a 200 million yuan loan.

WHY IT MATTERSThe paper trail, not a raid, produced the evidence.

The finding comes from collateral filings, not an enforcement action. Companies pledging hardware for loans must describe it, and the serial number on the GB300 rack let Bloomberg trace it to Gigabyte, the Taiwanese assembler that built it. Gigabyte declined to say whom it originally sold the rack to. That is how a system Nvidia's chief executive said in 2025 was too big to smuggle ended up documented inside China.

The ownership links run through Huang's network. Kezhan is part-owned by a subsidiary of 7Road, Megaspeed's original parent, with the rest ultimately owned by NeXPower Japan, a Tokyo firm Caixin reported is controlled by Huang. Shuoyao confirmed this month that it is one of Megaspeed's service providers. Megaspeed itself used a rendering of the project in 2024 to show investors its largest data center, without naming the country.

WHAT REMAINSDiversion is not proven, and the route is unknown.

Bloomberg reported no evidence that Megaspeed itself sent Nvidia hardware to the site, and Megaspeed has denied wrongdoing. Customs data points to one possible route: in May 2025, Kezhan received an $11 million shipment flown from Malaysia to Shanghai Pudong airport, declared as storage chassis at a unit price of $264,108, a figure that fits Hopper-based servers rather than storage.

What to watch next is whether the Commerce Department, which added Megaspeed to its trade blacklist this month, or the Justice Department, which is reported to be leading the investigation, acts on the filings; whether Gigabyte or any intermediary is named; and whether Nvidia's board, which reinstated Jensen Huang on November 23, responds to the first documented case of its flagship hardware inside China.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISThe smuggling question now has a serial number.

For a year the Megaspeed story ran on intelligence assessments and denials: US agencies concluded Huang acted as a Chinese intelligence asset, Nvidia's chief executive said he saw no evidence of diversion and that the systems were too big to smuggle. A loan filing changes the register. A serial-numbered GB300 rack, pledged as collateral by a Megaspeed-linked firm, is a document a regulator can hold, and it puts the flagship hardware of the AI boom inside China with a paper trail. What it does not do is close the case. No filing names the seller, the shipper, or the person who moved the rack, and the site's operators have not explained how it arrived. The finding raises the cost of every remaining denial, but the diversion itself is still unproven, and the next move belongs to the investigators, not the headlines.

WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAINWhat Remains Uncertain

The facts are as reported by Bloomberg, as read this round through TechSpot's full account of the Bloomberg findings and corroborated by Gizmodo, the New York Post and Decrypt, all citing the same Bloomberg reporting and the Chinese regulatory filings it reviewed. The filings show the hardware was at the site; they do not show who moved it there, and Bloomberg reported no evidence that Megaspeed itself diverted it.
