FAA Grounds All U.S. Departures in First Nationwide Stop Since 2023; New AI Routing Tool Faces Scrutiny

The FAA ordered a nationwide ground stop for all departing flights on Friday afternoon, November 13, 2026, after a telecommunications outage hit all 22 Air Route Traffic Control Centers; the advisory cited 'extraordinary circumstances' and safety, with about 11,000 flights carrying over 1.5 million passengers scheduled after 4 p.m. Eastern. It is the first nationwide halt since January 2023 and only the second since 9/11. Three days earlier the FAA said its AI tool SMART was active at all 22 centers. DHS Secretary Mullin said investigators will look at whether there is any link to the AI rollout; the vendor Air Space Intelligence denies any connection and no official has linked them. The FBI is looking into whether a software update triggered the failure and does not believe it was a cyberattack. WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

The Federal Aviation Administration ordered a nationwide ground stop for all departing flights on Friday afternoon, November 13, 2026, after a communications failure hit the air traffic control centers that guide planes between airports. The FAA's command-center advisory, issued at about 3:54 p.m. Eastern, said departures were halted 'due to extraordinary circumstances and for reasons of safety' and that all departing traffic should remain ground stopped until further notice. Inbound international flights were to be handled case by case, and planes already in the air were not ordered to land. It was the first nationwide halt of departures since January 11, 2023, when a computer-system failure grounded flights, and only the second since the aftermath of the September 11, 2001, attacks.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said the stop followed 'a communication issue impacting all Air Route Traffic Control Centers,' the 22 regional facilities that direct planes en route between airports, and that FAA technicians were investigating. The FAA later described the problem as 'a temporary telecommunications outage' and said flights were halted out of 'an abundance of caution.' The scale was immediate: about 11,000 flights carrying more than 1.5 million passengers were scheduled to take off from U.S. airports after 4 p.m. Eastern, according to Cirium, and at least 600 flights had been diverted by early evening, 496 back to their airport of origin and 104 to a different airport. By 5 p.m. Eastern, more than 8,000 domestic flights had been delayed and around 400 canceled.

The outage arrived three days after the FAA said its new AI-powered air traffic tool, SMART, short for Strategic Management of Airspace, Routes and Trajectories, was active in the airspace of all 22 of those same centers. The system, built by Air Space Intelligence under a 12-year contract valued at $875 million, pulls together about 200 data streams, including weather, flight paths, traffic flow and controller staffing, to recommend routes and schedules to controllers, who keep the final say. The FAA said it had rerouted 180 flights since the September 21 launch around Washington, D.C., saving an average of about 10 minutes and nearly 80 gallons of fuel per flight. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin told reporters that investigators will look at whether there is any link between the AI rollout and the outage, while adding that there was no indication yet of a nefarious attack and that the system operates out of one location near Washington, D.C., and only sends recommendations to the centers. Air Space Intelligence said on X that the ground stop was 'not based on or a result of the SMART system, any product of ASI or our integration with FAA and ATC.' The FBI is looking into whether a software update triggered the failure and does not believe the outage was caused by a cyberattack or criminal activity, according to Bloomberg.

WHY IT MATTERSA three-day-old rollout meets a failure it has not been shown to cause.

The timing is the story and also the trap. A nationwide communications failure landing less than 72 hours after an agency-wide AI deployment invites the obvious question, and the federal response so far has been to investigate the question without asserting an answer. SMART is an advisory layer, not a controller: it runs from one site, sends recommendations, and cannot move an aircraft. The FAA's own account of the failure is a telecommunications outage at the centers, not a routing recommendation gone wrong, and no official has linked the two events.

What the episode does expose is the readiness question around SMART itself. The controllers' union, which represents more than 15,000 members, told FLYING it was not involved in the system's design, testing or implementation, and lawmakers had already urged the FAA to pause its use for further evaluation. A system rolled out to the whole national airspace in weeks, over those objections, will now have its first weeks examined under the harshest possible light, whether or not it played any role in Friday's failure.

WHAT TO WATCHThe cause, the restart, and the review of SMART.

The next facts are the cause and the restart. By early evening the FAA said departures could resume in coordination with its command center as individual centers recovered, with the next command-center update due at 11 p.m. Eastern, but the Associated Press reported there was still no timeline for a full restart. The FBI's software-update line is the first concrete lead on a trigger, and any finding there will shape whether the failure was a routine telecom fault or a change-management failure.

The second watchpoint is SMART's own review. If the investigation clears the system, the FAA will still face the union and lawmaker questions about how it was deployed; if it finds any connection, the nationwide rollout becomes a case study in deploying AI into safety-critical infrastructure. The stop also coincided with Microsoft's global 365 outage, which was still open the same day; the Associated Press account does not connect the two events.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISThe question is not whether SMART failed. It is whether a new system was ready for the whole sky at once.

Tokenta Signal's judgment: the FAA's ground stop is a communications failure first and an AI story second, and the order of those facts matters. No official has linked SMART to the outage, the system only advises and cannot control aircraft, and the vendor's denial is on the record. What is genuinely new is the scrutiny: a $875 million AI system went nationwide in weeks, over the controllers' union's objections and lawmakers' calls for a pause, and its first test arrived as a nationwide failure three days later. If the investigation clears SMART, the deployment questions remain. If it finds a link, the rollout becomes the cautionary case for putting AI into safety-critical infrastructure. Watch the cause finding and the restart, not the speculation.
