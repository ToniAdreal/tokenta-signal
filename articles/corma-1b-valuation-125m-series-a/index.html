Corma Hits $1B Valuation Three Months After Launching Its Defense AI

Corma raised a $125 million Series A at a $1 billion valuation led by NEA, three months after emerging from stealth with a $60 million seed round. Total funding is now $185 million, and the company says demand from large enterprises surged after October's disclosure wave, with server capacity up 24-fold since August.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

Corma, a cybersecurity startup founded in 2025, raised a $125 million Series A at a $1 billion valuation led by NEA, the company said on November 16, 2026. The round comes about three months after Corma emerged from stealth in August with a $60 million seed round, bringing total funding to $185 million. Existing investors Sequoia Capital, Khosla Ventures and Coatue participated again; Sequoia led the seed.

Corma trains its own foundation models for cyber defense and deploys them as autonomous agents that work alongside security teams, using an organization's existing tools to investigate threats and carry out defensive tasks. The company says it stands apart by building models from scratch for defense, rather than layering a product over systems from OpenAI, Anthropic or DeepSeek, and describes itself as a frontier research lab focused on protection. It employs about 25 people across Tel Aviv and San Francisco, led by co-founder and CEO Alon Pluda, a graduate of the Psagot program and an alumnus of Israel's Unit 8200.

Demand surged after October 21, when critical flaws were disclosed in PHP and Node.js and the open-source tool LiteLLM reported a security incident, an episode the industry dubbed Black October. Corma says demand from Fortune 100, Fortune 500 and Fortune Global 500 companies has been overwhelming since, and it has expanded server capacity 24-fold since August to keep up. Globes estimated annual recurring revenue in the tens of millions of dollars, with customers in healthcare, financial services, energy, retail and telecommunications.

WHY IT MATTERSDefense is the new funding magnet

A $1 billion valuation three months after launch, for a company of about 25 people, shows how fast capital is moving toward AI defense. The backdrop is a market where AI has made attacks cheaper and faster: the same models that write the world's software can also find and exploit its flaws, and October's disclosure wave made that concrete for chief information security officers. Investors are now paying a premium for the other side of that trade.

The broader funding data points the same way. The Wall Street Journal, citing PitchBook, reported that investment in global cyber startups reached a combined $4.7 billion through September 2026, already ahead of 2025's full-year total of $3.8 billion. Corma is not alone in the space: Kevin Mandia's new startup Armadin is building in the same direction, and Anthropic, OpenAI and Microsoft all sell defensive tools. The question is not whether defense AI gets funded, but which approach wins.

WHAT TO WATCHThe claims to test

Corma's pitch rests on performance claims that have not been independently verified. The company says its models speed up some breach-response workflows by a factor of 100, and that one model outperformed GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5 on DefenseBench V5, an evaluation Corma built itself. No independent benchmark results have been published, and revenue is undisclosed, so the valuation rests on investor conviction and reported customer demand rather than public proof.

The second watch item is capacity. Pluda said securing compute fast enough to keep up with demand has been one of the company's hardest problems, and the new money is earmarked for more infrastructure and better models. If demand holds, the 24-fold capacity expansion since August is the start of a much larger buildout; if the October surge was a one-off scare, the valuation will face its first real test.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISA unicorn in three months, on defense

Tokenta Signal's judgment: Corma's $1 billion valuation three months after launch is the clearest signal yet that AI defense has become a funding priority in its own right, not a side bet. The October disclosure wave gave the pitch its proof point, and NEA, Sequoia, Khosla and Coatue all bought in. The open question is evidence: the 100x speedup and DefenseBench claims are self-reported, revenue is undisclosed, and the valuation assumes the surge in demand is durable. The next funding round, or the first independent benchmark, will settle which side of that bet is right.
