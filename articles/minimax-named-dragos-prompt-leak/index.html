MiniMax Named as the Lab Behind the Leaked AI Prompts

Dragos chief research officer HD Moore has publicly identified MiniMax as the Chinese AI developer behind his firm's investigation into AI data security. Secret links planted in prompts sent to MiniMax were opened days later from Singapore, appeared in a Feishu chat channel and were opened from Beijing and Shanghai, and drew unusual activity from the Moscow region. MiniMax says its own security system opened some links to check for threats and found no evidence customer conversations were disclosed.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

Dragos chief research officer HD Moore has publicly identified MiniMax as the Chinese AI developer at the centre of his firm's investigation into AI data security, Reuters reported on November 26, 2026. The November 19 Dragos investigation described the lab only as a Chinese AI developer; the identification is being reported for the first time.

Dragos sent nearly two million requests containing secret web links and test login details to AI services between July and November, then monitored whether anyone subsequently tried to access them. Private links embedded in requests sent to MiniMax were repeatedly opened two to three days later from an internet connection in Singapore, including when customers had requested that their information not be stored. Moore said the pattern appeared only when requests were forwarded to MiniMax's cloud, not when other companies ran its models independently.

One secret link sent to MiniMax appeared weeks later in a chat channel on Feishu, ByteDance's workplace messaging platform, and was opened by devices in Beijing and Shanghai. Researchers also detected unusual internet activity from the Moscow region involving links submitted to MiniMax, although the evidence did not establish that the links were opened from Russia. Dragos also detected attempts to download private collections of software code referenced in MiniMax requests, observed after the scraping of secret links appeared to stop on October 22; it is unclear who initiated the attempts or whether any code was obtained.

In a statement on Tuesday, MiniMax said its security system had collected and stored web links contained in messages sent to its AI models through other service providers, and had automatically opened some of those links to check for potential security threats. The company said it did not retain full conversations, had deleted the stored addresses, and had found no evidence, in the requests it examined, that customer conversations or AI responses had been disclosed to third parties. It did not address the attempted software downloads or the Feishu incident. US provider Atlas Cloud said it had stopped forwarding requests to MiniMax and warned customers who used its M3, M2.7 or M3.1 Flash models to treat any secrets submitted as potentially exposed.

WHY IT MATTERSThe naming turns a general warning into a specific accountability question.

Until now the Dragos findings were a warning about the AI supply chain in the abstract: secrets planted in prompts surfaced somewhere downstream, and nobody would say whose systems they surfaced in. Naming MiniMax, a Hong Kong-listed company whose models were among the three most widely used on OpenRouter in October, gives users, regulators and competitors a concrete party to question. It also matters because the exposure happened despite zero-retention requests, which undercuts the assurance many providers sell as a privacy guarantee.

The Feishu detail is the sharpest part. A test link did not merely get opened by an automated scanner; it appeared in a workplace chat channel weeks later and was opened from Beijing and Shanghai. That is the kind of chain-of-custody failure that turns a security-research finding into a governance question for every company routing confidential material through a third-party model.

WHAT REMAINSMiniMax's explanation answers the mechanism, not the whole finding.

MiniMax's account is narrow and specific: its own security system logged links and opened some of them to check for threats, it deleted the stored addresses, and it found no evidence of disclosure in the requests it examined. That explanation covers the Singapore openings. It does not cover the Feishu appearance, the Moscow-region activity, or the attempts to download private code collections, none of which MiniMax addressed in its statement.

Watch for three things: whether MiniMax issues a fuller account of the Feishu and code-download findings, whether other providers that forwarded MiniMax requests publish their own exposure notices, and whether Washington's consideration of disclosure rules for American companies using Chinese AI hardens into a requirement. Atlas Cloud has already stopped forwarding requests to MiniMax; OpenRouter removed three providers and tightened its data privacy requirements after the earlier findings.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISA named lab, an explained mechanism, and three unexplained findings.

The honest read splits the story in two. The naming is a real step forward: users now know which lab's systems were opening their secret links, and MiniMax's own statement gives a plausible mechanism for the Singapore openings, a security scanner that logged and opened links even on zero-retention requests. But the explanation stops there. A test link surfacing in a Feishu chat weeks later, unusual activity from the Moscow region, and attempts to download private code collections are not covered by a threat-scanning story, and MiniMax did not address them. Until those are explained, the prudent conclusion is the one Atlas Cloud already drew: treat any secrets sent to MiniMax models through third parties as potentially exposed, and route confidential work through providers whose data handling you can audit end to end.

WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAINWhat Remains Uncertain

The facts are as reported by Reuters on November 26, 2026, based on its interview with Dragos chief research officer HD Moore, and as stated in MiniMax's own Tuesday statement and the earlier Dragos investigation of November 19, with corroboration from Traders Union, WinBuzzer and Security Boulevard, all read this round. The identity of the parties behind the Feishu appearance and the Moscow-region activity has not been established, and MiniMax disputes that any customer conversations or responses were disclosed.
