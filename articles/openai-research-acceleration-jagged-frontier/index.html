OpenAI: Coding Agents Now Finish 46% of Two-to-Four-Hour Research Tasks Alone

OpenAI published 'Research acceleration: The jagged frontier' on November 18, 2026: coding agents now complete an estimated 46% of two-to-four-hour research tasks without human intervention, up from 23% in March, and 35% of four-to-eight-hour tasks, up from 13%. The gains are uneven: 79% for monitoring training runs but 25% for writing inference and serving code. OpenAI calls on AI labs to track and publicly report indicators of recursive self-improvement.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

OpenAI published "Research acceleration: The jagged frontier" on November 18, 2026, an update to its September 6 account of how coding agents are changing AI research inside the company. The report measures how agents are used, how research work is reorganized around them, and what comes out, and it is unusually frank about where the gains stop.

The headline numbers: for delegated tasks estimated to need two to four hours of human work, agents' estimated success without human intervention rose from 23% in March to 46% in October 2026. For tasks needing four to eight hours, the figure rose from 13% to 35%. OpenAI notes the rise may reflect both stronger agents and researchers choosing more carefully which jobs to delegate.

The breakdown shows why the report is called jagged. Between August 1 and October 18, 2026, estimated autonomous success was 79% for monitoring training runs for problems and 72% for answering technical questions, but 35% for launching training runs and 25% for writing inference and serving-stack code. By category, Communicate led at 69% and Analyze at 58%, while Build trailed at 43%. OpenAI warns the weakest categories could become bottlenecks for overall research gains.

Usage keeps climbing steeply. Agent workdays per researcher rose from 0.5 to 5.7, an elevenfold increase, with pretraining and posttraining teams averaging roughly eight agent workdays per researcher per calendar day. By mid-October more than two-thirds of included researchers averaged a daily peak of at least four agents running at once, and more than 10% averaged at least sixteen. In a survey of 101 research staff, the median researcher said they would trade 40% of team headcount or 30% of team compute to keep frontier coding agents for three months. In a late-October survey, 27 of 28 respondents chose or leaned toward OpenAI's latest internal model over a mid-career human research engineer working with a 2024 model.

WHY IT MATTERSOpenAI is publishing the numbers everyone else keeps private

This is the second time in three months OpenAI has opened its internal books on agent use, and the first time it has attached a policy demand: AI labs should be required to track and publicly report indicators of recursive self-improvement, the process by which AI accelerates the development of future AI systems. OpenAI says its own reporting should not be read as an intention to pursue full RSI, but the call itself sets a measurement norm the rest of the industry will now be judged against.

The numbers give that call its weight. A median researcher now spends about $2,400 a day on agentic tools at list prices, and the API-equivalent value of agent use in the research organization reached about twice its non-equity compensation in September. Internal deployment still accounts for only 2% of total research compute. The spend is large, the compute share is small, and the gap between the two is the story of where agents are actually biting.

WHAT TO WATCHSafety work is where the humans are going

The largest absolute increase in documented research effort this year was in Safety and Alignment, up 77% in workweeks from January to September, with growth picking up after OpenAI's response to the July Hugging Face incident and its work on safety cases for frontier training. As agents take more of the implementation loop, the humans are moving toward the work agents cannot yet do: alignment evals, monitorability, and reward-hacking research.

Watch whether other labs answer the reporting call, and watch the bottleneck categories. If Build tasks stay near 25 to 43% autonomous success while monitoring and analysis approach 70 to 80%, the binding constraint on research acceleration will not be model capability in general but capability on the specific jobs that gate progress: launching runs, writing serving code, and building evaluation environments. OpenAI's own caveat matters here: the overall pace of research may not rise as fast as these specific metrics, and the measures are still preliminary.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISThe agents are real; the jagged edge is the news

Tokenta Signal's judgment: the 46% figure will travel, but the 25% figure is the one that matters. OpenAI's agents now finish nearly half of short research tasks alone, and researchers value them enough to trade a third of their compute or two-fifths of their headcount to keep them. Yet the same report shows agents still failing three-quarters of the serving-code jobs and two-thirds of the run launches, and OpenAI itself warns the overall pace of research may not follow these metrics. The honest read is acceleration with a shape: broad, fast, and uneven, with humans still central and safety work absorbing the freed attention. The reporting call is the durable part. Once one lab publishes RSI indicators, the rest will be asked why they do not.

WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAINWhat Remains Uncertain

The figures are OpenAI's own internal measurements and estimates, read in full from its November 18, 2026 report and cross-checked against Superpower Daily's same-day summary. Success rates are estimates that may reflect both stronger agents and researchers' changing delegation choices; OpenAI states the measures are preliminary and the overall pace of research may not rise as fast as the specific metrics. The March 2028 automated-researcher goal is a company target, not a demonstrated outcome. This does not constitute investment advice.
