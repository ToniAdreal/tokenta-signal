Neuralink Wins FDA Approval for First Pivotal Brain-Implant Trial in ALS

Neuralink received FDA approval to launch the LINK Pivotal Study on November 25, 2026, the first pivotal trial for an implantable brain-computer interface in the United States. The study will evaluate the safety and effectiveness of the Neuralink system in individuals with severe paralysis caused by ALS, building on early feasibility studies in the United States, Canada and the United Kingdom with more than 30 implanted participants. Enrolment is now open.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

Neuralink received approval from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration to launch the LINK Pivotal Study on November 25, 2026, the first pivotal trial for an implantable brain-computer interface in the United States. The study will evaluate the safety and effectiveness of the Neuralink system in individuals with severe paralysis caused by amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, known as ALS.

The study builds on Neuralink's ongoing early feasibility studies in the United States, Canada and the United Kingdom, where more than 30 implanted participants have contributed thousands of hours of real-world use. Participants have used their implants to pursue higher education, express their creativity, play video games and communicate with loved ones.

The LINK Pivotal Study will test whether participants can control digital devices through thought, with a focus on functional outcomes that improve independence in daily life, and will continue to assess the long-term safety and reliability of the system across multiple clinical sites. The system uses the N1 implant, placed in the brain's motor cortex, with Neuralink's R1 robot inserting fine electrode threads. Enrolment is now open to adults with ALS in the United States.

WHY IT MATTERSFrom feasibility to the trial that decides market access.

Early feasibility studies show a device can work in a handful of people. A pivotal study is the trial that decides whether a device can reach the market at all: it is designed to generate the safety and effectiveness evidence the FDA weighs for marketing authorisation. Neuralink moving to this stage means the regulator considers the evidence base strong enough to test at scale, with formal endpoints and multiple clinical sites.

The ALS focus is deliberate. The disease strips away voluntary movement while leaving cognition intact, so a working thought-to-device interface is not a convenience but a route back to communication and independence. If the study shows reliable cursor control and daily-task performance, the same platform extends to spinal injury and stroke, the populations in Neuralink's earlier studies.

WHAT REMAINSApproval to run a trial is not approval to sell a device.

The distinction matters and Neuralink's own announcement keeps it: the FDA authorised the study, not the product. Enrolment numbers, participating sites and study duration have not been disclosed, and the trial will run for years before any marketing application. The 30-plus participants so far are a thin base for a pivotal claim, and long-term safety of a permanently implanted device is exactly what the study must prove.

The competitive clock is also running. Synchron's endovascular approach and other BCI programmes are pursuing the same patients with less invasive surgery. Neuralink's edge is bandwidth from a direct cortical implant; its burden is proving that the surgical risk is worth it, in a trial the FDA will read closely.

THE SHIFTWhat Changed

FROM FEASIBILITY TO PIVOTAL
From feasibility studies to a pivotal trial
Neuralink's work so far has been early feasibility studies with a small group. A pivotal study is the formal trial that generates the evidence for a marketing decision, a step change in regulatory standing.
FROM CURSOR CONTROL TO DAILY INDEPENDENCE
From cursor control to daily independence
Earlier demonstrations centred on moving a cursor. The LINK study's stated focus is functional outcomes that improve independence in daily life, a higher bar that decides whether the device matters outside a lab.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISThe implant works in thirty people. The trial decides whether it works for everyone.

Neuralink has spent years proving a brain implant can let a paralysed person move a cursor. That was the feasibility question, and thirty participants answered it. The pivotal study is a different question: whether the device is safe and effective enough, across many sites and many patients, to be sold as a medical product. The FDA saying yes to the trial is a serious signal, but it is not a yes to the device. Watch enrolment pace and the endpoints Neuralink publishes; a trial that fills fast and measures daily independence, not just cursor speed, is the one that changes this field.
