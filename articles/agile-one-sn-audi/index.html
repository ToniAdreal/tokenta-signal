A 1.4-Metre Humanoid Heads to Audi to Handle Parts First

Agile Robots and Nvidia unveiled the Agile ONE SN research humanoid at GTC Berlin: 1.4 metres, 71 degrees of freedom, tactile five-finger hands. The Audi pilot has it handling about 1 million picking and sequencing cycles a day.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

On October 21, Agile Robots and Nvidia unveiled the Agile ONE SN, a humanoid robot for research, at the GTC conference in Berlin; on October 27, a press release disclosed further details of its pilot project with Audi. Two days earlier, Nvidia had announced at a Berlin investment summit that it planned to invest in Agile Robots.

The Agile ONE SN stands 1.4 metres tall, weighs 43 kilograms and has 71 degrees of freedom; both hands are WUJI tactile five-finger hands, it runs on Nvidia's Jetson Thor, and it comes preloaded with the GR00T 1.7 open vision-language-action model. Its positioning is explicit: not a production machine, but a reference platform for research labs worldwide.

The first application stop is the production lab at Audi's Ingolstadt plant: Agile Robots, Audi, Dassault Systèmes and Nvidia jointly announced that a mobile humanoid will pick parts from containers and sequence them for final assembly, a task repeated roughly 1 million times a day. The work cell is being tested first in a DELMIA virtual twin before entering the real line.

THE PLATFORMResearch Machines First Is the Pragmatic Humanoid Route

What the Agile ONE SN sells is not the robot itself but transferable skills: skills developed on the SN can migrate to the full-size industrial Agile ONE. Research machine first, industrial machine after, with the skill asset as the link between them.

That differs entirely from the consumer humanoid route of selling complete machines directly: seed the platform into labs first, and let researchers worldwide build out the skill library for you.

THE HANDSTactile Five-Finger Hands Are This Machine's Real Selling Point

Of the 71 degrees of freedom, the most notable are the hands: WUJI tactile five-finger hands. For picking and sequencing tasks, the difficulty is not walking but force control and tactile feedback in grasping.

The Diana 7 and Diana 3 robot arms released at the same time point the same way: ±0.05 millimetre repeatability and 0.5 newton relative force-control precision, with support for the Franka control interface, so Franka Research 3 code can migrate directly.

THE TESTAudi's 1 Million Repetitions Are the Humanoid Proving Ground

Picking and sequencing 1 million times a day is the most honest examination room for a humanoid: repetitive, high-frequency and measurable. The success standard is not dexterity in a demo video but the stability rate over continuous operation.

Running it in a virtual twin first, then entering the real line, is itself the answer: the path for humanoids into factories passes through simulation validation before any talk of replacing human labour.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISHumanoids Enter Factories by Passing Simulation First

The Agile ONE SN's cleverness lies not in its height or degrees of freedom but in its sequence: research platform first, virtual twin first, measurable repetitive tasks first. Tokenta Signal's judgment is unchanged: the humanoid production narrative is shifting from launch-stage demos to reproducible factory rhythm.

WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAINWhat Remains Uncertain

The boundaries of this report: the Agile ONE SN is a humanoid reference platform for research use, with deliveries only planned for early 2027; the Audi project is currently at the virtual-twin testing stage, not a production line in operation. This does not constitute investment advice.
