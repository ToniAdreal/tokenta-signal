Meta Plans to Raise Another $95 Billion for AI

The funding plan Meta announced on October 26 has two parts: a launched offering of about $35 billion, and a proposed at-the-market programme for up to $60 billion of Class A common stock. Together they total roughly $95 billion, earmarked for AI infrastructure and personal superintelligence investment. The company also guided 2026 capital expenditure of $135 billion to $145 billion.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

The launched portion has a clear structure: about $22.5 billion of Class A common stock under forward sale agreements, generating no cash inflow initially, and about $12.5 billion in mandatory convertible preferred stock depositary shares. The proposed $60 billion at-the-market programme has not been finalised and is expected to start in the first quarter of 2027 at the earliest. Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom expressed interest in subscribing for up to $5 billion of Class A common stock in total, on a non-binding basis.

The backdrop is a scissors gap between cash flow and spending: Meta generated $97 billion in operating cash flow in the first three quarters, while full-year capital expenditure guidance has reached $135 billion to $145 billion. The plan describes fundraising capacity, not cash in hand; cash under the forward agreements only arrives at future settlement.

THE STRUCTURERead the Structure Before the Scale

The $95 billion figure is easily mistaken for money in the bank. It actually has three parts: Class A shares under forward sale, preferred stock depositary shares, and an at-the-market programme that has not been finalised. Only the preferred portion corresponds to a relatively certain issuance; cash from the forward portion and the at-the-market plan depends on later execution.

For readers, the key distinction is one: launched versus proposed. The $35 billion launched is an offering under way; the $60 billion is a planned capacity. The two cannot simply be added together and treated as funds raised.

THE GAPThe Gap Between Cash Flow and Spending

Operating cash flow of $97 billion in the first three quarters against full-year capex guidance of $135 billion to $145 billion: the gap itself is the origin of the funding plan. The pace of AI infrastructure spending has outgrown what the company's own cash flow can cover, and external financing has shifted from an option to a maintenance requirement.

The chipmakers' subscription interest deserves a separate look: Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom, up to $5 billion combined, and non-binding. A supplier willing to become a shareholder is a different thing from a supplier willing to cut prices.

THE SIGNALThe Funding Plan Is Itself a Statement

Meta has not trimmed its plans in response to market questions over AI spending; it has put the funding capacity on the table instead. Management's judgment is plain: the entry ticket to the compute race keeps getting more expensive, and stopping costs more than borrowing.

The risks are equally plain: an at-the-market programme dilutes existing shareholders, and settlement prices under the forward agreements depend on the future share price. Fundraising capacity is not fundraising completed, and planned capacity can shrink if market conditions change.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISThe Pace of Spending Matters More Than Its Scale

Meta's funding plan does not answer when AI investment pays off. It answers a more practical question: the investment cannot stop. Tokenta Signal's judgment is unchanged: the compute race will be decided not by who announces the biggest number, but by whose cash flow lasts until the returns arrive.

WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAINWhat Remains Uncertain

The boundaries of this report: the $60 billion portion of the funding plan has not been finalised and is not expected to start before the first quarter of 2027 at the earliest; the chipmakers' subscription interest is non-binding; the timing of cash inflows from the forward sale of Class A common stock depends on future settlement arrangements.
