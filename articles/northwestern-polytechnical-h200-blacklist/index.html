Blacklisted Chinese University Bought Nvidia H200 Chips, Records Show

Reuters reported on November 11, 2026 that Northwestern Polytechnical University, a PLA-linked Chinese university on the US Entity List since 2001, bought two Nvidia H200 chips for about $123,000 through a domestic supplier, and rented access to restricted Nvidia processors through five contracts this year. A review of 26 procurement records points to a wider market for restricted chips at Chinese universities.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

A Chinese university with deep ties to the People's Liberation Army bought Nvidia's powerful H200 AI chips last month despite being on a US export blacklist, procurement records show. Northwestern Polytechnical University, a member of China's "Seven Sons of National Defense" group of defence-focused universities, bought two H200 chips for 874,700 yuan (about $123,000), according to an October 21 procurement record on its website.

The chips were bought for the university's management school from Shaanxi Bosuan Network Technology, a domestic supplier, rather than directly from Nvidia. The H200 is among Nvidia's most capable chips for training and running advanced AI systems and is subject to US export licence approvals, which are normally denied for blacklisted institutions such as Northwestern Polytechnical.

The university also bought access to H200 chips and other powerful Nvidia processors through five contracts for rented computing capacity this year. Its cybersecurity school awarded two seven-month contracts in January for access to servers containing eight H200 chips each, worth 441,700 yuan and 441,000 yuan, covering fine-tuning AI models and reinforcement-learning training. It also rented access to servers containing eight Nvidia A100 chips and eight A800 chips.

WHY IT MATTERSThe blacklist did not stop the purchase

The US Commerce Department added Northwestern Polytechnical to its trade blacklist, known as the Entity List, in 2001 over alleged involvement in missile-proliferation activities and military research. It tightened the restrictions in 2019, making licences for exports of controlled US technology to the university subject to a policy of denial in most circumstances. The university conducts research related to military aircraft, missiles and satellites.

The Trump administration began allowing some H200 exports to China on a case-by-case basis in January, but that policy did not lift the separate restrictions applying to Entity List organisations. The January licensing rules also require safeguards to prevent blacklisted entities and military end-users from remotely accessing chips exported under the policy. Buying through a domestic supplier, rather than from Nvidia directly, is how the purchase went around those rules, and the records offer scant information about where the processors originated.

WHY IT MATTERSRenting is the quieter channel

The outright purchase is only part of the picture. A Reuters review of 26 Chinese procurement and corporate records from this year found universities and research institutions buying US-restricted Nvidia chips or renting access to Nvidia-powered computing services through domestic suppliers. Several had close links to China's defence industry or military research.

The wider market is not small. Shenzhen University bought three H100 processors, another high-end Nvidia AI chip banned by the US, for 769,800 yuan in September. Wuhan University of Technology bought a server containing an H100 chip for 355,000 yuan in June and four A100 chips in a separate 344,000 yuan purchase. Beihang University, another of the Seven Sons, bought four A800 processors for 489,000 yuan in April. Two government procurement announcements in July and September named suppliers providing computing services using restricted H800 and A800 chips to the University of Chinese Academy of Sciences, one listing H800 access at 10 yuan per chip-hour.

The findings land as Nvidia and chief executive Jensen Huang face mounting scrutiny in Washington over his lobbying for looser restrictions on AI chip sales to China. Both Democrats and Republicans in Congress have urged stronger controls, and three Republican congressional committee chairmen have requested a briefing with Huang on November 18 over his reported contacts with an alleged Chinese intelligence operative.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISThe controls are leaking at the edges

Tokenta Signal's judgment: this is not a story about one university buying two chips. It is a story about a channel. The outright purchase is small, $123,000, but the rental contracts, the five suppliers, and the 26 records showing the same pattern across universities are the signal. Export controls assume the chokepoint is the sale; the procurement records show the market moved to domestic intermediaries and rented compute instead. The briefing Huang faces on November 18 is now about more than his contacts. It is about whether the control regime still controls anything.

WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAINWhat Remains Uncertain

The figures come from Reuters' review of Chinese procurement records, read in full, and cross-checked against independent republications of the same Reuters exclusive. The university, Nvidia, and the US Bureau of Industry and Security did not respond to Reuters' requests for comment, and the records do not show where the processors originated or where the rented servers operated.
