Tesla Doesn't Want to Build Cars Anymore

Tesla did not 'go all in on robots' with a wave of the hand: it killed the Model S and X not because robots won, but because the EV profit story can no longer be told. A 4.6% operating margin cannot support a trillion-dollar valuation. A $25 trillion pie can.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

On January 28, 2026, Tesla's Q4 earnings call: full-year 2025 revenue of $94.8 billion, down 3% year on year; net income of $3.8 billion, down 46%; operating margin shrunk from a peak of about 17% to 4.6%. On the same call, Musk announced the end of Model S and Model X production — the two lines at the Fremont factory will be converted to build the Optimus humanoid robot and the Cybercab.

Killing two flagship models is unheard of in automotive history. But the numbers explain everything: when selling cars earns a 4.6% margin, while robots in Musk's telling are a '$25 trillion market opportunity', the production lines must make way for the new story. This is not a heroic amputation; it is sacrificing the rook to save the king.

THE MARGIN COLLAPSE4.6%: the end of the EV profit story

Tesla was once the world's most profitable carmaker, with operating margins near 17% that Toyota and Volkswagen could only look up at. In 2025, that number is 4.6%. Under the triple squeeze of price wars, tariffs and peaking demand, electric vehicles have gone from money printers to a thin-margin business.

The trend is crueller still: $94.8 billion in revenue is still shrinking, and $3.8 billion in net income has halved. When 'selling cars' is no longer sexy, capital markets will not award a high valuation for a 4.6% margin. Tesla must tell a new story — and Optimus and the Cybercab are its two protagonists.

THE PRODUCTION GAP15,000 units versus zero: the production Rashomon

At March's Abundance Summit, Musk confirmed Optimus V3 would 'begin mass production in the summer'; supply-chain rumours put 2026 production at about 15,000 units, with weekly output near 1,000 by end-September and a long-term Fremont target of 1 million a year. But on the July Q2 earnings report, Tesla itself admitted: the first-generation line is being installed, cumulative output is still roughly zero, and the first machines are going to the internal 'Optimus Academy' for training data.

15,000 units in words, roughly zero on the books. That is the state of Tesla-style production: targets always grand, deliveries always 'next year'. The specs look beautiful — 173cm tall, 57kg, 22 degrees of freedom in one hand, the AI5 chip, Grok voice, a target price of $20,000-30,000 — but specs are not products. Shipments are.

THE BET$20 billion: the most expensive pivot in history

Tesla's 2026 capex exceeds $20 billion, a record, going to six new or converted production lines and Giga Texas's Cortex AI training cluster (about 85,000 Nvidia H100/B200 GPUs). In essence, this money switches all of a car company's capital expenditure onto the AI and robotics track.

The logic is called 'dual-robot platform economics': the Cybercab and Optimus share the same AI5 chip, FSD vision stack and Cortex compute. In the first half of 2026, within weeks of each other, both crossed from 'pre-production' into 'delivery' — the Cybercab joined Austin's public robotaxi fleet on September 4. One platform, two products, amortising the same R&D cost.

THE SHIFTWhat Changed

FROM AUTOMAKER TO ROBOTICS COMPANY
From car company to robotics company
When the most profitable business becomes a thin-margin one, the company's identity must be rewritten. Tesla is rewriting its first principles.
FROM SHIPPING CARS TO SHIPPING NARRATIVE
From shipping cars to shipping narrative
15,000 units or zero does not matter right now — what matters is what kind of future capital markets are willing to pay for.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISThe carmaker is dead; long live the robot

When a car company's $20 billion in capital expenditure all flows to AI and robotics, and the lines of its flagship models are converted into robot lines, the sentence 'Tesla is a car company' is already past tense. Only one question remains: can the new story's delivery speed outrun the old story's collapse?

WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAINWhat Remains Uncertain

If Optimus's real deliveries are still close to zero at the end of 2026, and the Cybercab's seven-city expansion keeps slipping, the 'robotics company' narrative turns from asset to liability — the premium investors paid for 'unlimited upside' will demand real shipment volumes in return. Musk is best at painting the pie, but this time the pie has a shelf life of one year.

EVIDENCEEvidence
