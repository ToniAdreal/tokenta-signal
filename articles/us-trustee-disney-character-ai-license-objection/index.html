U.S. Trustee Asks Court to Kill Disney's $20M Character.ai License Deal

The U.S. Trustee, the Justice Department's bankruptcy watchdog, asked the Delaware bankruptcy court on November 24, 2026 to deny Character Technologies' request to sell Disney a license to its technology for $20 million. In a 23-page objection (Docket No. 232, Case No. 26-11603-BLS), the Trustee argues the deal's core, Disney's hiring of 66 of the company's 86 employees including its chief executive and the legal releases that came with it, was completed on September 18, before the October 12 bankruptcy filing, and the court has no power to approve it after the fact. The filing also says nobody valued the released claims before they were given up and asks where $2.7 billion received in August 2024 has gone. Only the license goes to auction on December 1, with Disney's $20 million as the floor; Judge Brendan L. Shannon is scheduled to hear the sale on December 7 and 8.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

The U.S. Trustee, the Justice Department official who oversees bankruptcy cases in Delaware, asked the federal bankruptcy court on November 24, 2026 to deny Character Technologies' request to sell Disney a license to its technology for $20 million. In a 23-page objection, Docket No. 232 in Case No. 26-11603 (BLS), the Trustee argues that the core of the deal was completed on September 18, 2026, before the company filed for Chapter 11 on October 12, and that the court has no power to approve it after the fact.

On September 18, Disney signed an exclusivity and prepayment agreement with Character Technologies, paid $5.5 million non-refundable plus a $2 million refundable deposit, and hired 66 of the company's 86 employees, including chief executive Karandeep Anand. The legal releases took effect on signing: Character.AI promised never to pursue claims against Disney and the people it hired, including claims over Disney's recruiting of its staff and over work those employees do at Disney that draws on what they built at Character.AI. The Trustee says those claims stopped belonging to the company once given up, so they were not part of the estate when it filed. "Nothing in the Bankruptcy Code or the Bankruptcy Rules authorizes a debtor to seek ex post facto approval of a transaction consummated pre-petition," the objection says.

The filing also says nobody valued the claims before they were given up: the independent directors who approved the deal had not reviewed the value of the claims being released, and any review began only after signing and has not concluded. The Trustee further asks where $2.7 billion the company received in August 2024 has gone. Only the license itself goes to auction on December 1, with Disney's $20 million as the floor and a $700,000 break-up fee if a rival wins. The Trustee says that auction cannot test the price of what Disney actually got, because the hiring and the releases are not on the block. Judge Brendan L. Shannon is scheduled to hear the sale on December 7 and 8.

WHY IT MATTERSThe watchdog is attacking the deal's timing, not its price.

This is the U.S. Trustee's fourth objection in the case, and the first aimed at the sale itself rather than its procedures, its banker's fee, or the second bidder. The earlier objections were mostly overruled. This one rests on a narrower legal claim: a bankruptcy court can approve the sale of property the company owned when it filed, but it cannot retroactively bless a transaction that was already finished weeks before the filing.

If the judge agrees, the consequences reach beyond the $20 million license. The proposed sale order would deem the September releases valid, protect Disney as a good-faith purchaser, and shut the door on any later challenge to what happened on September 18. The Trustee is asking to keep that door open, so the creditors' committee or a future liquidation trustee can still examine the hiring, the releases, and the roughly $180 million in compensation and retention that flowed to departing employees rather than to the estate.

THE SECOND MOVEThe Disney deal now faces opposition from both the states and the federal watchdog.

The Trustee is not the first to object to Disney's deal. On November 18, a coalition of 39 states and the District of Columbia asked the court to deny it on overlapping grounds, and on November 24 at least 42 states moved to block the separate sale of the consumer platform and its users' private chats. The Trustee adds a distinct argument about what a bankruptcy court is allowed to approve, plus the unanswered question about the $2.7 billion.

The December calendar now carries two tests. The auction on December 1 decides whether any rival tops Disney's $20 million for the license and Smirks and Pangs' $10 million for the platform and user data. The sale hearing on December 7 and 8 decides whether either deal survives the objections. The judge has already shown he will overrule objections to the sale procedures, so the Trustee's timing argument is the sharpest challenge the Disney deal has faced.

THE SHIFTWhat Changed

FROM PRICE TO TIMING
From a price fight to a timing fight
The Disney deal was framed as a $20 million license with a $700,000 break-up fee. The Trustee's objection turns it into a fight over dates: the hiring and the releases happened on September 18, the bankruptcy came on October 12, and the court is being asked to approve the first with the powers of the second.
FROM PROCEDURE TO SUBSTANCE
From procedural objections to the sale itself
The Trustee had objected three times before, to the bidding rules, to the investment bank's fee, and to the second bidder, and lost the fee fight on November 23. This filing is the first aimed at the sale itself, and it arrives alongside the states' objections to both halves of the deal.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISThe Disney deal's biggest risk is now a calendar date, not a rival bid.

A $20 million floor price was never the hard part of the Disney deal; the September hiring was. The Trustee's objection attacks the one fact Disney cannot reprice: the releases were signed and the employees moved before the bankruptcy existed. The judge has overruled the watchdog before in this case, on the banker's fee and on the sale procedures, so an objection alone does not sink the deal. But this filing asks a question no rival bid can answer, whether a court can approve after the fact what was already done. If the answer is no, the December 1 auction tests a license stripped of the protections Disney bargained for, and the sale hearing on December 7 and 8 becomes the real contest.
