3.44 Million Chips Requested, 879,000 Approved: The Export Ledger Goes Public

The export-control ledger is open for the first time, and the numbers are more restrained than the rumors.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

On November 19, 2026, the House Foreign Affairs Committee published the Bureau of Industry and Security's export licensing records. From December 2025 to May 2026, companies applied to sell 3.44 million AI chips to Chinese buyers; 879,000 were approved, about a quarter. The models were Nvidia's H200 and AMD's MI325X.

Approved buyers included Tencent with 150,000 chips, Alibaba with 75,000, Baidu with 43,000 and BYD with 4,000; all four sit on the Pentagon's list of Chinese military companies. Nvidia had also applied to sell 610,000 chips to Alibaba and 508,000 to ByteDance; neither large order was approved.

Approval is not delivery. The records show that as of May 11, 2026, not one of the approved chips had shipped. Nvidia interim CEO Colette Kress said on the earnings call that the company has stopped shipping H200s to China. A new rule taking effect the same day also brought data-center services offering remote use of advanced chips into licensing scope.

WHY IT MATTERSA Quarter Approved

879,000 against 3.44 million: the licensing picture is stricter than outsiders imagined. The big orders mostly failed; Nvidia's two applications above 500,000 chips each, to Alibaba and ByteDance, were both rejected.

But Tencent's 150,000 chips were approved in full, which says licensing is not a blanket refusal. The committee published the ledger so the public can see which buyers got through and which did not.

WHAT TO WATCHThe Timing of the Disclosure

Committee chairman Brian Mast used the disclosure provision of the Export Control Reform Act to lay confidential licensing records open. Before publication, Jensen Huang was set to face congressional questioning; the ledger amounts to evidence placed on the table first.

Treasury Secretary Bessent then said existing controls are sufficient and no new rules are needed. The remote-use rule taking effect the same day extends controls from the chips themselves to data-center services, tightening the opening further.

THE SHIFTWhat Changed

FROM SECRET LICENSES TO PUBLIC LEDGER
From secret licenses to public ledger
Export licensing records were confidential until the committee used the disclosure provision to open them. Licensing now has numbers that can be checked, and rumors give way to the ledger.
FROM APPROVALS TO SHIPMENTS
From approvals to shipments
879,000 chips approved, not one shipped. Between a license and a delivery sit shipping intent and new rules; the number itself is not the end of the story.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSIS3.44 Million Chips Requested, 879,000 Approved: The Export Ledger Goes Public

Tokenta Signal's judgment: The export-control ledger is open for the first time, and the numbers are more restrained than the rumors. The real dividing line is not how many chips were approved, it is that not one shipped.

WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAINWhat Remains Uncertain

Approved quantities follow the licensing records; actual shipped quantities have not been published. List membership follows the Pentagon's designation, and the companies named may not agree with it.
