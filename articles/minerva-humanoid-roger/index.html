This Humanoid Isn't Taking Jobs. It's Taking the Place of the Dying.

Minerva Humanoids raised a $10 million pre-seed and is not competing to fold laundry or work factory lines. It builds exclusively for bomb disposal, oil and gas and high-voltage power stations — the one humanoid lane nobody else contests. Semi-autonomous, teleoperated by VR: not taking people's jobs, but taking their place in harm's way — with paid pilots starting this autumn.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

On October 6, 2026, Minerva Humanoids announced its emergence from stealth: roughly $10 million in pre-seed funding, led by General Catalyst with Long Journey Ventures and Credo Ventures as co-leads, and a follow-on list that includes Hugging Face and Codecademy. The company was founded only in late 2025 — less than a year ago.

The founding team is a 'dream team' on paper (per the company's press release): CEO Sandor Felber previously led the MIT CSAIL product line using humanoid robots for AI research and worked on Tesla's Optimus programme; CTO Maurice Rahme spent six years at Boston Dynamics as one of the technical leads on the Stretch robot, taking it from early prototype to commercialisation. The team also includes Dominic Asquith, a former British special forces soldier, heading Hazardous Operations.

What makes Roger different is the phrase 'semi-autonomous': it is designed for the world's most dangerous jobs — offshore and onshore oil and gas operations, high-voltage power stations, explosive ordnance disposal (EOD) and hazardous-materials response. An expert wears a VR headset and teleoperates the robot, performing fine manipulation through the robot's 'eyes'. In the company's words: 'Human judgment and hands on the equipment, the body stays in the command post.'

The speed story is equally striking: from project kickoff to a walking humanoid in just five months, including custom design, manufacturing and assembly. The first prototypes were built and assembled in Germany, with production planned in Germany and the US, emphasising 'no single-source components from China' — a full-throated Western supply-chain narrative. GC partner Robin Dechant said: 'Building a humanoid from scratch is often a years-long project, but Sandor and Maurice did it in less than six months.'

THE LANE NO ONE FIGHTS FORNot folding laundry, defusing bombs: the lane nobody fights for

While every humanoid robot is competing to fold laundry and work factory shifts, Minerva chose the one lane nobody contests: bomb disposal and oil and gas platforms. Felber's line set the narrative in stone: 'People are dying every day doing jobs the rest of the world takes for granted. We didn't set out to replace the people doing them.' Not replacing people — going to die in their place.

The demand figures are Minerva's estimates, extrapolated from ILO/IEA data: one death every 75 minutes in the global energy industry, roughly 7,000 a year. NATO ranks EOD and hazmat response among the highest-risk tasks for any unit — existing tracked, single-arm bomb-disposal robots 'lack dexterity', and when they cannot finish the job, a human still has to put on the bomb suit and make 'the long walk'.

Asquith, the former special forces soldier, put it vividly: 'Every EOD operator knows the long walk. When the robot can't complete the task and the operator has to put the EOD suit on and approach the device alone.' When the robot fails, the human walks alone toward the bomb — ending that long walk is what Roger is for.

TELEOPERATION, HONESTLYSemi-autonomy plus VR: the industry's most honest technology route

Minerva's smartest move is refusing to oversell full autonomy. In a year when every humanoid company claims 'general intelligence', it openly states that its core scenarios rely on experts teleoperating via VR headsets: the robot goes, the human judges.

That honesty is precisely why it can charge money. The first paid pilots start this autumn — note: paid pilots, not free trials. In bomb disposal, high-voltage stations and oil platforms, customers are not buying 'intelligence'; they are buying 'no human needs to be there'. Semi-autonomy is not a technical compromise; it is product honesty: save lives first, upgrade intelligence later.

The partnership map is already drawn: demonstrations at NATO's EOD Centre of Excellence in Slovakia; research collaborations with the UAE's MBZUAI (energy scenarios) and the University of Waterloo (perception in visually denied environments such as smoke, darkness and sensor failure); and deployment talks with two of the world's ten busiest airports, with on-site tests completed. Airports, energy and EOD are all 'one accident makes the news' high-risk settings — exactly where willingness to pay is strongest.

MADE WITHOUT CHINAMade in Germany: the supply chain as a declaration

The first prototypes were manufactured and assembled in Germany, with production planned across Germany and the US and 'no single-source components from China' — in a humanoid industry deeply penetrated by Chinese supply chains, that sentence is itself a political declaration.

The calculation is precise: the customers are the NATO ecosystem, energy giants and airports — buyers far more sensitive to supply-chain security than to price. 'Western-made' here is not a cost disadvantage; it is a bidding qualification.

But the other side of the coin is cost: building a walking machine in five months is a speed myth; the production ramp is another matter. Can a humanoid produced on German and US lines bring unit cost down to a level where energy and defence customers sign without blinking? The first scaled orders after the paid pilots will answer that.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISNot taking jobs. Taking the place of the dying.

While the whole industry tells the story of 'general-purpose humanoids replacing labour', Minerva tells the opposite story: not replacing people, but dying in their place. Bomb disposal, high-voltage stations, oil and gas platforms — customers in these settings are not buying intelligence; they are buying 'no human needs to be there'. This is the humanoid industry's first scenario that can actually charge money, because the ROI here is not efficiency. It is human life.

WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAINWhat Remains Uncertain

A note of caution: the founders' track records, the five-month build and the estimate of roughly 7,000 deaths a year all come from the company's own press release; there is no independent third-party verification yet. Whether paying pilots renew — and whether they scale after renewal — is the company's real exam. However good the story sounds, it only counts once the invoices go out.
