House Duo Introduces Bill Forcing Frontier Labs to Report AI-Driven Research and Self-Improvement

Representatives Josh Gottheimer (D-N.J.) and Nathaniel Moran (R-Texas) introduced the RSI Safety Act on November 24, 2026, a bipartisan bill requiring the largest AI developers to file quarterly reports with a new Commerce Department office on how much of their research is conducted by AI and how much is reviewed by humans, and to notify the government if their systems begin developing independently. It would cover developers that spent at least $100 million on training in the past year, create the Office of Frontier Artificial Intelligence Research, and expose developers to up to $10 million in civil liability for false or missing reports.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

Representatives Josh Gottheimer (D-N.J.) and Nathaniel Moran (R-Texas) introduced the RSI Safety Act on Tuesday, November 24, 2026, a bipartisan bill that would require the largest artificial intelligence developers to report to the federal government how much of their research and development is being carried out by AI systems, Politico reported. Bloomberg Government independently confirmed the introduction the same day, reporting that developers would also have to notify the government if they notice their systems beginning to develop independently.

The bill would require covered developers to file quarterly reports with a new Commerce Department office on how much of their research is conducted by AI and how much of that work is reviewed by humans. It would apply to developers training models that reach a certain advanced level of computing power and who spent at least $100 million in total on training in the past year.

The new office would be called the Office of Frontier Artificial Intelligence Research and would be overseen by a director appointed by the Commerce secretary. It would publish aggregate reports for the relevant congressional committees and give developers guidance on what form public summaries of their safety, evaluation, and oversight operations should take. The Center for AI Standards and Innovation, renamed by the Trump administration as the Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence, would assist on measurement rules and the design of auditable metrics. The bill also includes whistleblower protections and would expose developers to as much as $10 million in civil liability for false or missing reports.

WHY IT MATTERSReporting, not a ban: Congress tests a disclosure regime for self-improving AI.

Recursive self-improvement, AI models training successor models with diminishing human involvement, has moved in a week from a research concern to a legislative program. The RSI Safety Act does not ban the practice or cap automation. It makes the practice visible: quarterly figures on how much frontier research is done by AI, how much humans still review, and a duty to flag the moment a system starts developing on its own.

That disclosure-first design is deliberate. The sponsors frame the bill as a roadmap for Congress on whether limits on these training methods are needed, which puts measurement before restriction and gives the new Commerce office, not the labs, the job of publishing aggregate findings. If the reporting shows automation climbing steeply, the same figures become the evidence base for the harder measures already circulating.

THE SECOND MOVEThe bill lands in a crowded field, and the spectrum now runs from disclosure to prohibition.

The RSI Safety Act is the third congressional move on self-improving AI in six days. Representative Lori Trahan's revamped FRONTIER Act, outlined November 19, adds transparency and auditing requirements for recursive self-improvement, and a separate SAFE AI Act outline from Representatives Sara Jacobs, Celeste Maloy, and Don Beyer the same day would require frontier developers to disclose substantially autonomous AI research. Governor Gavin Newsom's California framework, released November 18, goes further, proposing a standby state cap on recursive self-improvement.

At the other end of the spectrum, Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Greg Casar want to ban the training method altogether, a position Senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed has backed. The Gottheimer-Moran bill sits between the two poles: mandatory reporting with civil penalties, no development limits. Its fate will show whether Congress treats self-improving AI as a transparency problem first or moves straight to the prohibition debate.

THE SHIFTWhat Changed

FROM VOLUNTARY TO MANDATORY
From voluntary disclosure to mandatory quarterly reporting
Earlier proposals relied on voluntary agreements or state-level recommendations. The RSI Safety Act would make reporting on AI-conducted research a federal duty for the largest developers, with civil penalties for false or missing reports.
FROM CONCERN TO LEGISLATION
From a research concern to a legislative program
Recursive self-improvement was a topic for evaluator reports and safety frameworks. Within six days it produced a California framework, two House outlines, and a bipartisan bill, with a full prohibition proposal at the far end of the spectrum.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISDisclosure first, limits later: Congress wants the numbers on self-improving AI before it decides what to ban.

The RSI Safety Act is the most concrete federal step yet on recursive self-improvement, and its design is the message. Rather than capping automation or banning the training method, Gottheimer and Moran would force the largest labs to quantify it every quarter and flag the moment a system starts developing on its own. That gives Congress a measurement regime it has never had, and it gives the labs a choice they should take seriously: report honestly now, or hand the prohibition camp the evidence it needs later. The bill's real test is whether quarterly self-reporting, backed by $10 million penalties and whistleblower protections, produces numbers anyone trusts. If the figures look thin, the debate will not stay at disclosure for long.
