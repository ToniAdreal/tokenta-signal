Three Incursions Into Work Zones, and Waymo Pulls Freeway Rides Nationwide

The hard part of self-driving is not ordinary traffic, it is the exception: the work zone.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

On November 17, 2026, Waymo announced a nationwide pause on driverless freeway operations. California's Department of Motor Vehicles had ordered on November 11 that Waymo stop all driverless operations and testing on the state's freeways, citing repeated incursions into active construction zones. Waymo extended the restriction to its other markets on its own; surface streets keep running.

Two incidents were the immediate trigger. On November 5, three Waymo driverless vehicles entered a closed construction area on Highway 101 in Burlingame. On November 6, a Waymo vehicle tried to pass a California Highway Patrol convoy conducting traffic control, and video of the moment spread on social media. Waymo said neither incident caused injuries or collisions.

This is not the first time. Waymo only began offering freeway service late last year, paused once in May 2026 after a construction-zone incident, and resumed in July after a software update. This time the DMV's notice states plainly that there was already one restriction and one round of fixes, and the problem kept coming back. The ban has no expiry date; it stays until Waymo produces fixes that pass the DMV's review.

WHY IT MATTERSThe Ban Has No Expiry Date

The DMV's wording is the tell. It found Waymo's driverless freeway operations an imminent hazard to public safety: workers, vehicles and equipment in a work zone leave almost no margin for error. Responding with a ban rather than a fix-it notice is the regulator saying the earlier remedy did not convince it.

Waymo's answer was to widen the halt voluntarily. Surface streets, more than 500,000 trips a week, are unaffected, and supervised freeway testing continues. The posture is generous; the real test comes later, when the fix has to persuade the DMV that the same incursion will not happen a third time.

WHAT TO WATCHThe Freeway Is the Hardest Stretch

The difficulty of the freeway is not speed, it is the exception. Temporary detours in a work zone, cones, a trooper's hand signals: none of it sits in the standard map, and the model has to read a human's improvised arrangements on the spot. Waymo failing twice in the same place says that capability is not yet in place.

No one can give a recovery timetable now. The DMV wants to see effective fixes, and Waymo has to produce something more persuasive than the July update. Until then, the freeway stretch stays empty.
