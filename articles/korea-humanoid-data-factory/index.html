South Korea opens its first humanoid robot data factory: 12 robots do the work first, and bank the data

South Korea's Ministry of Science and ICT unveiled its first bipedal-humanoid data factory on November 3 at KIMM headquarters in Daejeon. Built by KIMM and ETRI, it runs 12 remotely operated robots collecting task data in its first batch, plans to expand to 25, and will open in phases to companies, universities and research institutes from the end of 2027. The project sits in the K-Moonshot humanoid track, and the government has set aside 2.3 trillion won for the humanoid industry before 2030.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

South Korea's Ministry of Science and ICT unveiled the AI Humanoid Data Factory on November 3 at the Daejeon headquarters of the Korea Institute of Machinery and Materials (KIMM). Built jointly by KIMM and the Electronics and Telecommunications Research Institute (ETRI), the facility covers about 1,000 square metres over two floors, runs 12 bipedal humanoid robots in its first batch, and plans to expand to 25.

In the unveiling demonstration, researchers remotely operated humanoid robots to fetch items from a refrigerator, handle laundry, tidy a sofa, and work buttons and levers inside a car. Every movement was recorded to train vision-language-action models, the trained models were then loaded back into the robots for testing, and the test data flowed back into training — a closed loop of collection and training.

The project benchmarks itself directly against China. Interact Analysis counts at least 90 humanoid data-collection and training centres in China operating or under construction as of the end of April, at least 64 of them running, and 13 deploying more than 100 robots. In late October, Shanghai announced it had built the world's first heterogeneous humanoid embodied-intelligence training ground in Pudong, having collected more than 50,000 hours of real-robot multimodal data.

The South Korean government has placed the factory in the humanoid track of its K-Moonshot programme. On August 27 the Ministry of Economy and Finance set aside 2.3 trillion won (about $1.7 billion) for the humanoid industry before 2030, including procuring 1,080 domestically made humanoid robots and lifting the localisation rate of core components from about 45% to 80%. Version 1.0 of KIMM's own humanoid robot KAIROS is set to debut in April 2027, and the factory will open in phases to companies, universities and research institutes from the end of 2027.

WHY IT MATTERSThe humanoid race's bottleneck is not the body, but the data

Humanoid hardware is not short of players; what is missing is the data that teaches it to work. Language models have internet text to learn from; the physical world has no ready-made record to copy, so every grasp, door opening and laundry fold must be produced by a real machine in a real environment. Whoever scales collection first, their model learns general tasks first — which is why South Korea classifies the data factory as national infrastructure.

South Korea's play is national-team first: government funding, research institutes building the plant, data circulating inside the system first, then opening to industry from the end of 2027. That pace is slower than China's, where more than 60 centres are already running and single centres with a hundred robots are not rare. South Korea's 12-robot start is more like an entry ticket; the real test comes after expansion to 25 — whether collection speed can catch up, and whether KAIROS can deliver a usable 1.0 in April 2027.

WHY IT MATTERSThe opening timetable matters more than the unveiling

The unveiling is only step one. Who the data belongs to, under what licence, and whether outside teams can get the raw trajectory data — none of this has been published. The official South Korean wording is phased opening of the facility to companies, universities and research institutes, which is a different thing from a public dataset: the former is booking a slot to enter, the latter is download-and-use, and the speed of industry spillover differs by an order of magnitude.

For Chinese readers, the reference value of this story is methodological: when a rival industrialises data collection with a national budget, the news value of single-point model breakthroughs gives way to collection capacity. The next verifiable progress will not come at a press conference but at two 2027 milestones: whether KAIROS 1.0 ships on time, and who the first outside teams into the factory are.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISThe data factory is the humanoid race's real battlefield

Tokenta Signal's judgment: the right way to read this story is to treat the unveiling as a starting point. Twelve robots and 1,000 square metres are not large; the real information is South Korea's admission that the deciding factor in humanoid intelligence is the industrial-scale production of task data, not a single robot's demonstration. With China's 60-plus running centres as the comparison, South Korea's catch-up clock started on November 3, and the April 2027 KAIROS 1.0 and the year-end opening list are two verifiable tests.

WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAINWhat Remains Uncertain

The facts in this report are based on Seoul Economic Daily's November 3 report and Aju Press' November 3 report, both opened and verified directly, with the core figures in agreement. 'Korea's first' follows the framing of KIMM and the Ministry of Science and ICT, meaning the first facility in South Korea dedicated to data production for bipedal humanoid robots. The robots in the unveiling demonstration were remotely operated by humans; the demonstration tasks do not equal autonomous operation already achieved. Opening to outside users from the end of 2027 is a planned timetable, and the openness and licence terms for the data and models have not been published.
