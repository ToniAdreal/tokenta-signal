UK Parliament Demands Anthropic Hand Over the Unredacted METR Safety Report

Liam Byrne, chair of the House of Commons Business, Innovation, Science and Trade Committee, wrote to Anthropic on November 27, 2026 demanding the full, unredacted version of METR's November 5 assessment of AI research and development acceleration at the company, with an explanation of why the evidence was cut. WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

Liam Byrne, who chairs the House of Commons Business, Innovation, Science and Trade Committee, wrote to Pip White, Anthropic's head of UK and Ireland, on November 27, 2026. The letter asks for a full and unredacted copy of METR's November 5 report on how fast AI research and development was accelerating inside Anthropic in October, and an explanation of the basis on which the redactions were made. The committee wants an answer by Friday, December 4. The Decoder first reported the letter on November 28, and multiple outlets have since confirmed its contents.

METR, the nonprofit evaluator, spent 11 weeks inside Anthropic, from August 12 to October 29, 2026, and concluded that AI assistance had roughly doubled the pace of the company's own research since October 2025, with about 80% of the speed-up attributed to AI itself. Its central estimate put the acceleration at about 1.9 times, with a 95% confidence interval of 1.5 to 2.8 times. The report also warned of a substantial risk that AI development at Anthropic could speed up further, by a factor of 1.3 to 3, with no clear bottlenecks in sight.

Anthropic held the right to redact non-public information from the published report, and used it heavily. METR said Anthropic redacted a majority of the evidence it found most informative for its conclusions, including the detailed attribution of acceleration across the training pipeline, the split between compute, data and algorithmic progress, and forecasts of future models. Anthropic said the cuts were aimed at removing confidential information that could accelerate other AI developers, and co-founder Sam McCandlish confirmed the redactions were meant to protect sensitive commercial details.

WHY IT MATTERSA legislature asking for the evidence, not the conclusion.

The committee is not disputing METR's finding. It is asking for the material behind it. That distinction is what makes the letter consequential: the published report already says Anthropic's AI is speeding up its own research by roughly twofold, and the argument since November 5 has run between people who could not see the evidence. A parliament with standing, a deadline and an open inquiry is now asking for the missing pages on the record.

The request also tests the cooperative model of AI oversight. The UK has relied on voluntary access through its AI Security Institute rather than legal compulsion, and a Commons committee has no power to force documents out of a San Francisco lab. What it can do is publish, embarrass and recommend. The reply, due December 4, will show whether Anthropic hands over the report, offers a private briefing, or refuses, and each answer sets a precedent for how the next such letter is treated.

WHAT REMAINSThree ways this can end, and only one puts the evidence in public.

The most likely outcomes, as observers note, are a flat refusal, a confidential handover, or a one-time reading-room arrangement for safety-institute staff. Only a public release would close the gap METR described. A confidential handover would leave the public record exactly where the November 5 report left it, with the most informative evidence still unseen.

The durable question is statutory. The committee's inquiry into AI adoption, safety and governance, opened on September 30, is the likeliest vehicle for recommending real access powers for Britain's safety institute. Until such powers exist, letters like this one are requests, not orders, and the December 4 reply will be read as a measure of how far voluntary cooperation stretches when the evidence is uncomfortable.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISThe letter is a request, not an order, and that is precisely the test.

Tokenta Signal's judgment: the substance of this story is not the letter but the hole it points at. METR's public report already establishes the headline, that Anthropic's AI is doing roughly twice the research work it did a year earlier, and most of the acceleration comes from AI itself. What is missing is the evidence for why, stage by stage, and where it goes next. Parliament cannot compel it, and Anthropic has a defensible reason to withhold commercial detail. But a frontier lab that publishes a redacted safety report and then declines to show the redactions to a legislature invites the inference that the redactions protect more than trade secrets. The December 4 reply matters less for what it contains than for what it signals: whether voluntary oversight still works when the evidence is uncomfortable, or whether the UK concludes it needs powers it does not yet have.
