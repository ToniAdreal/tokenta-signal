Bessent: Nvidia Still Has Not Answered on China Chip Sales, Seven Days Into 10-Day Deadline

Seven days into Bessent's 10-day deadline, Nvidia has delivered no answers on its China chip sales, and the Treasury Secretary wants to know who ran compliance.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

On November 22, 2026, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told CBS's "Face the Nation" that the Trump administration still has no answers from Nvidia about its chip sales to China, seven days into a 10-day deadline he set the previous Sunday. "We've been in touch with the temporary management, with the lead directors, with their lawyers, and we do need answers," Bessent said. "It behooves Nvidia to move quickly on this because we want to know what the culture was." Asked by host Margaret Brennan whether he had received the answers he demanded, Bessent replied: "We don't, Margaret."

Bessent said the administration wants to know who was in charge of compliance at the company formerly led by Jensen Huang, who resigned after reports of an affair with a woman US intelligence suspects of being a Chinese spy, and an extramarital affair with a company executive. "We do need to see who was in charge of compliance there," Bessent said. "We will see which level of the business knew about this. Was it at the lower levels? Was it at the middle level? Did it go all the way to the top?" He added that the board is moving quickly and he has confidence it will produce "some very fulsome and robust answers."

The pressure follows a week of overlapping scrutiny: investigations by US regulators and the FBI, congressional action, an internal review and a proposed investor class-action lawsuit. Days after Huang's resignation, the administration issued a rule further restricting China's access to advanced AI chips, and the House Foreign Affairs Committee released documents showing the Commerce Department had approved the sale of more than 800,000 Nvidia and AMD AI chips to companies with Chinese ties, including two linked to the Chinese military. Bessent said the president has drawn a hard line at H200-class chips. President Trump said on Friday that he would like Huang to apologize and return to Nvidia; Bessent said more information is needed first: "I think we have to see where the buck stops." Board Chairman Harvey Jones has said the board will not rehire Huang.

WHY IT MATTERSA culture question, not a one-man question

Bessent's framing on Sunday deliberately widened the inquiry beyond Huang himself. Brennan pressed him on the point, noting that a "culture of non-compliance" is "not just the foibles of one man," and Bessent answered that the possibility is what the board must now establish or rule out: "Was there promiscuous attitude toward this, and where, if it happened, were the faults." He also separated the compliance question from the personal scandal, calling the relationship with the alleged spy "kind of a canard" next to the question of whether the company's culture tolerated illicit sales, while adding that he assumes Huang did not know she was an alleged spy.

The mechanics of the inquiry cut against one excuse in advance. Brennan raised reporting that Nvidia's deals were often based on handshakes, leaving little paper trail; Bessent replied that deals of that size are traceable anyway: "There are bills of lading that movement. There are money movements, so it will be possible to understand what has gone on here." He said compliance would normally sit with the legal department and the general counsel's office, and that the administration will see "what they have to say." His other line, that the answer may be "malicious actors down at the bottom of the company who were responsible for the non-compliance," keeps every level in scope at once.

WHAT TO WATCHThe Megaspeed file and the remote-access rule

Watch what the board delivers before the deadline runs out in three days, and whether it names anyone in compliance or the general counsel's office. The Singapore company at the center of the affair, Megaspeed, was taken off Nvidia's vendor and sales list in August 2025, so it has received no chips since then, Bessent said, and Treasury and several other departments have pushed to blacklist it while it is investigated as a possible pass-through to Chinese entities. Senator Tom Cotton, the Republican chair of the Intelligence Committee, told the Wall Street Journal he is frustrated that Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick ignored his repeated letters about Megaspeed, and said the administration must now "catalog the damage."

Watch also the enforcement tool Bessent called more important than a blacklist: the remote-access rule issued last week, which bars certain countries and entities from reaching advanced chips through leased capacity, covering Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil. On Friday, Bessent told CNBC that no further restrictions on older Nvidia chips are needed and "the proper chip restrictions are in place," so the administration's posture is now enforcement of the existing line rather than new curbs. The test of that posture is the board's answer, and whether the "fulsome and robust" response Bessent expects actually identifies who knew what, at which level.

THE SHIFTWhat Changed

FROM PERSON TO CULTURE
From a one-man scandal to a culture inquiry
The Huang affair began as a story about one executive's conduct. Bessent's Sunday appearance reframed it as a question about the company's compliance culture and which level of the business knew what, with the board now expected to answer for the system, not the individual.
FROM RULES TO ANSWERS
From new curbs to enforcement of the line
On Friday Bessent said no further restrictions on older Nvidia chips are needed. On Sunday he pressed for answers instead, pointing to the new remote-access rule as the enforcement tool that matters, a shift from writing rules to proving the existing ones were followed.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISBessent Presses Nvidia on Compliance Culture as Deadline Nears

Tokenta Signal's judgment: The news is not a new accusation but a clock running out. Bessent set a 10-day deadline a week ago and used a second Sunday show to say, on the record, that nothing has arrived. The substance of his questions is narrow and answerable: who ran compliance, at what level did knowledge sit, and was there a culture of pushing sales that should not have happened. The board's answer, due within three days, will be judged against that framing, and the remote-access rule gives the administration an enforcement lever that does not depend on proving what any one executive knew. What matters next is whether the response names names and levels, or offers a process answer to a responsibility question.

WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAINWhat Remains Uncertain

Bessent's remarks describe a possibility, not a finding: no regulator has concluded that Nvidia broke export-control law, and the company has said its limited H200 shipments were legal and authorized.
