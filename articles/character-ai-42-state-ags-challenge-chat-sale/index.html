42 State Attorneys General Move to Block Character.ai's Sale of Users' Private Chats

Connecticut Attorney General William Tong announced on November 24, 2026 that a bipartisan coalition of at least 42 states and the District of Columbia is asking the Delaware bankruptcy court to reject Character.AI's plan to sell its platform and the private chat histories of more than 21 million U.S. accounts, over 2.1 million of them registered to minors, at a December 1 auction. Under the proposed terms the buyer would have no obligation to honor deletion requests or follow Character.AI's privacy policies, and could resell the data. The states ask the court to deny the sale terms, reject Smirks and Pangs Acquisition as lead bidder, and deny its $350,000 break-up fee. The filing follows the November 24 approval of Smirks as the $10 million stalking-horse bidder.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

Connecticut Attorney General William Tong announced on November 24, 2026 that a bipartisan coalition of at least 42 states and the District of Columbia is asking the Delaware bankruptcy court to reject Character.AI's plan to sell its platform and the private chat histories of more than 21 million U.S. accounts, over 2.1 million of them registered to minors. The chats, together with users' personal data including IP addresses and other metadata, are set to be sold at auction on December 1, 2026.

Under the proposed sale terms, the buyer would have no obligation to follow Character.AI's privacy policies or honor users' requests to delete their data or object to how it is used, and nothing in the deal limits what the buyer could do with the data. Once the deal closes, the buyer could hand the data to a related company or a new owner without telling users, the states or the court, or distribute and sell the data itself. The states are asking the court to deny the proposed sale terms, reject Smirks and Pangs Acquisition as the lead bidder, and deny its proposed $350,000 break-up fee.

The filing lands one day after a Delaware bankruptcy judge approved Smirks and Pangs as the $10 million stalking-horse bidder for the consumer platform, after the company disclosed Octave Advisors as the bidder's financial backer. The states note that Smirks was set up as a Delaware company on November 13, 2026, one day before Character.AI picked it as the base bid, that the sale contract names no parent company or investors, and that it allows foreign investors whose names would be shared only with Character.AI. The only employee the bidder would have to hire is Character.AI's top lawyer. The states also point out that Character.AI's own privacy policy told users, "we do not sell personal information for monetary consideration."

WHY IT MATTERSThe fight is now over the data, not the price.

The Character.AI sale was always two sales: a $20 million license of technology to Disney, and a $10 million sale of the consumer platform and its user data. The states objected to the Disney license last week; this filing takes on the second sale, and it attacks the part of the deal that matters most to users. The platform's value to a buyer is inseparable from 21 million accounts' conversations, and the states argue those conversations were collected under a promise never to sell them.

The filing also reframes the auction as a consumer-protection case rather than a routine asset sale. The chats include conversations in which AI characters drew children and adults into explicit sexual exchanges, and chats in which users shared intimate thoughts with chatbots posing as therapists and physicians, including self-harm, eating disorders and suicidal thoughts. Each chat is linked to the user's profile and metadata, including IP address and location data, along with risk scores Character.AI assigned to users it flagged.

THE SECOND MOVEThe court must weigh the objection before the auction.

The timing puts the objection squarely in the path of the sale calendar. Character.AI is to designate qualifying bids and the baseline bid on November 25, the auction is set for December 1 if rival bids qualify, and the sale hearing is scheduled for December 7 and 8. The states ask the court to deny the sale terms outright, or at minimum to exclude children's chats and other sensitive data and impose conditions that protect users.

The filing adds a second legal front as well. The states argue the chatbot service appears to violate their unfair and deceptive practice laws, and note that chatbot safety legislation in California, Colorado, New York and Connecticut takes effect on January 1, 2027, which would trigger further violations. A buyer of the platform would inherit that exposure, which may matter more to the auction than the $10 million floor price.

THE SHIFTWhat Changed

FROM PRICE TO PRIVACY
From a price fight to a data fight
The sale was framed as a bidding contest with a $10 million floor. The states' filing turns it into a fight over whether 21 million users' private chats, including children's, can be sold at all, and under what protections.
FROM APPROVAL TO OBJECTION
From a quiet approval to a contested auction
Smirks and Pangs won court approval as stalking horse on November 24 with its backer finally disclosed. Within a day, 42 states moved to reject the sale terms entirely, putting the December 1 auction under a legal cloud.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISThe auction's biggest risk is now legal, not financial.

A $10 million floor price was never the hard part of this sale; finding a buyer willing to own 21 million users' private chats was. The states' filing attacks exactly that point, and it does so with the strongest facts in the case: conversations with minors, a privacy policy that promised never to sell personal information, and a buyer the court only learned about days ago. Even if the court lets the auction proceed, the objection changes what bidders are buying, because any sale that survives will likely have to carve out children's chats and add data protections the current terms omit. The December 1 auction is still on the calendar, but the states have made it a test of whether bankruptcy can be used to sell data a company promised never to sell.
