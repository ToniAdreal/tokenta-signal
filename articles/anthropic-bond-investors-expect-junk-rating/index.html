Bond Investors Expect Anthropic's Debt to Be Junk, Despite a $1.18 Trillion Valuation

The Wall Street Journal reported on November 23, 2026 that bond-fund managers expect Anthropic's debt to be rated junk, landing in the BB range, despite a roughly $1.18 trillion valuation. The company has committed about $900 billion to computing power and infrastructure, with about $447.5 billion due by the end of 2030. No formal rating has been published by Moody's, S&P or Fitch; the only agency rating on record is Kroll's BB+ from June 2026.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

The Wall Street Journal reported on November 23, 2026 that bond-fund managers expect Anthropic's debt to be rated junk, or below investment grade, despite the company's roughly $1.18 trillion stock-market value. Several large bond-fund managers told the Journal they expect Anthropic to land a rating in the BB range, a notch or two short of investment grade, which would make it by far the most valuable high-yield company in the United States. Hilton Worldwide currently holds that distinction, according to Dow Jones Market Data, with a market value of around $80 billion.

The expectation follows the numbers in Anthropic's amended S-1 filing: aggregate commitments of about $895.4 billion to $900.9 billion, substantially all for computing power and infrastructure, with about $447.5 billion due by the end of 2030 and about $70.7 billion due within twelve months of September 30, 2026. Anthropic reported $17 billion of revenue last quarter, against $1.4 billion a year earlier, but still lost money, and it has no other business to give bond investors comfort. Its $96 billion in cash on hand, plus the proceeds from the IPO and a concurrent private-share placement, can fund the commitments for a year or more, analysts told the Journal.

No agency has ruled yet. Spokespeople for Fitch Ratings and S&P Global Ratings declined to comment, a spokeswoman for Anthropic declined to comment, and the big ratings firms could deliver a split decision. The only agency rating on record is Kroll Bond Rating Agency's BB+, assigned in June 2026. Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei said on CNBC on Thursday: "Obviously, at some point in the future, we're hoping to be investment grade." "Any Anthropic debt deal is going to be viewed with a suspicious eye," said Craig Manchuck, a bond-fund manager at Osterweis Capital Management.

WHY IT MATTERSThe equity market and the bond market disagree about the same company.

Equity investors value Anthropic at about $1.18 trillion, above Walmart or JPMorgan Chase, after it raised more than $100 billion last week. Bond investors, who receive interest payments and no upside, are asking a different question: who pays $900 billion. The gap is not unique to Anthropic. CoreWeave, another large-cap AI company, also carries a junk rating, and Apollo chief executive Marc Rowan told a Journal podcast in October that companies with $40 billion to $60 billion market capitalizations were paying 16% to 18% for their debt, adding: "One of those things is wrong."

The Journal notes the counter-precedent. Bond markets were also skeptical of Tesla in 2018, just before the company, and its share price, took off. The difference is the clock: roughly half of Anthropic's commitments do not start until 2030, and without an investment-grade rating by then, the company could struggle to pay its bills. The rating expectation is not a rating, but it sets the price of the next dollar Anthropic borrows, and that price decides whether the compute build-out stays affordable.

THE WATCHPOINTThe funding route is already shifting away from plain bonds.

An investment-grade rating would open the $8.6 trillion high-grade corporate bond market, a relief for chip makers: Broadcom and Nvidia have guaranteed tens of billions of dollars of loans to buyers of chips rented to AI labs, and analysts have grown worried about circular financing deals. The junk-bond market is relatively small at $1.7 trillion and carries high borrowing costs, so fund managers told the Journal the company is unlikely to issue many unsecured bonds.

Instead, they expect Anthropic to keep accessing capital through structured financing and chip financing, "where there are ways to enhance their own credit quality," said Brian Barnhurst, head of global credit research at PGIM. "They have about three years of runway to get investors particularly comfortable with the credit story and their plan, so that the capital markets remain open for them," said Neil Sun, a bond-fund manager at RBC Global Asset Management. The company has also started buying its own computing hardware, assets that could ease the way to an investment-grade rating; SpaceX landed one thanks to its satellite business. The honest watchpoint is the first public debt issue, which will force a formal rating, and the first quarterly report as a public company.
