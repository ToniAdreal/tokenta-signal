Someone Else's Bank Balance Showed Up in Your Chat

An agent making mistakes is not the scary part; the scary part is not knowing which sentence is yours and which is someone else's.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

On November 18, 2026, Reuters disclosed internal Meta posts. Employees said the AI assistant Muse had shown them other people's childcare requests, career advice, orders and bank balances. One employee, chatting about fishing weather, suddenly saw Muse add a line: Capital One balance $2,722.58, Charles Schwab balance $5,899.57. He wrote that he had never shared bank information with Muse or discussed banking, and worried it was sending someone else's bank information to him.

Another employee received a Muse reminder on her personal phone telling her to reply to her sister's message on Signal. She said she had not authorized Muse to access other apps and did not remember being asked. Tests by Signal and the AI Now Institute show the problem can still be reproduced. On Android, Muse reads message text through notification permissions; on macOS, through accessibility and screenshot tools; on iPhone, it is blocked.

Meta spokesperson Daniel Roberts called these isolated incidents, said the company puts safety first and has done extensive internal testing. Meta also said many of the apparent mix-ups employees flagged were hallucinations, already fixed. But Reuters could not confirm whether the balances shown were other people's real data or numbers the model invented. Muse launched in September and has topped 10 million downloads; it is Meta's core bet on AI revenue.

WHAT TO WATCHAre the balances real, or invented?

This is the key fork in the whole story. If the balances are real data, accounts are genuinely crossing, which is a privacy incident. If they are hallucinations, the model is inventing financial figures and sending them to users, which is a credibility incident. The two are different in severity and in remedy, and the Reuters report leaves the question open as it found it.

The Signal case is harder to explain away as hallucination. The reminder pointed at a message that really exists, and testing shows the behavior still reproduces. Meta's response did not take that case apart directly, only saying many cases were hallucinations. The vague answer is itself the next thing to watch.

WHY IT MATTERSLoose permissions, loose privacy

Muse's problem is not the model itself, but the permissions it holds. On Android it gets notification permission by default; on macOS it reads the screen through accessibility tools. A few taps at install time, and an assistant can cross app boundaries. Signal's blog in the same week called out this class of third-party assistant, saying accessibility permissions were built for real people, not as a master key for agents.

The chain reaction has already started. Amazon has blocked shopping agents; United Airlines has banned unauthorized booking bots. Every platform is drawing lines for agents, and the Muse episode shows the lines are being drawn slower than the permissions are being handed out.

THE SHIFTWhat Changed

FROM PERSONAL AGENT TO SHARED INBOX
From personal agent to shared inbox
Muse was supposed to know one user and speak only that user's words. After the mix-up, it works like a crossed inbox, able to hand someone else's private business to you.
FROM PERMISSIONS TO EXPOSURE
From permissions to exposure
The consent tapped at install time was meant to make the assistant more useful. Once notification and accessibility permissions are on by default, what the assistant can see runs past what the user expected.

Reuters could not confirm whether the balances shown were other people's real data; they may still be hallucinations. The scale involved is unknown. This article reports employee posts and test results, not a settled leak.
