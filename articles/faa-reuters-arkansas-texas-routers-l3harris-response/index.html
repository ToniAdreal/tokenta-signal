WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

Update to flashes 358, 359 and 365: government officials told Reuters on Saturday, November 14, 2026, that the nationwide aviation shutdown may have been the fault of a single router installed by government contractor L3Harris, and that there may have been problems with routers installed in both Arkansas and Texas. The new detail widens the picture from the Saturday morning briefing, where Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy described a router flooding the FAA network during copper-to-fiber maintenance at a remote facility. The overload hit communications at 17 of the FAA's 22 en route centers across the country, and the FAA ordered the nationwide ground stop because it could not identify a root cause in the moment.

The routers being replaced are about 25 years old, Duffy said, and were supposed to be replaced in 2006. The FAA spends $1 billion a year maintaining its old copper wire system while racing to replace it with fiber-based communications. The flood sent more than 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds through the network, sparking communications issues with planes and radar at FAA facilities. The FAA said communications did not go down outright but led to instability in the data at the centers, and that the centers did not go dark. More than 20,000 flights have been disrupted since Friday, according to Reuters.

L3Harris has now responded, after declining to comment at the Saturday briefing. The company said it took the issue seriously and was supporting the FAA in its investigation to understand the conditions that led to the November 13 ground stop, and noted it has completed more than 70% of the planned conversion from legacy copper wires to a fiber-optic system. Duffy sharpened his criticism of the contractor, saying, "We need L3Harris or whoever provided that router to us to do a better job," and noting that virtually every institution in America has moved from copper to fiber and analog to digital. He said the FAA would reevaluate when maintenance work is done at smaller facilities to prevent a repeat. Congress has already provided $12.5 billion to upgrade the air traffic control and telecom system, and Duffy has asked for $50 billion more. The formal root-cause finding is still pending, and officials continue to say they do not believe a cyberattack caused the outage.

WHAT TO WATCHTwo states, not one facility

The Saturday briefing described a router failing during maintenance at a remote facility, singular. The Reuters account adds that officials see possible problems with routers in both Arkansas and Texas, and that the flood reached 17 of the 22 en route centers. The geography matters: if the failure touched two states, the question of whether it was one bad router or a bad batch of 25-year-old hardware becomes harder to contain to a single installation error, and it strengthens the case that the system was overdue for replacement years ago.

Watch for the FAA to name the facilities, for L3Harris to say more about the routers it supplied, and for the formal investigation to settle whether the fault was installer error, faulty hardware, or both. The $50 billion funding request Duffy is pressing now rests on a failure that occurred inside the modernization work itself, and the routers at the center of it were supposed to have been replaced in 2006.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISThe failure is widening, not narrowing

Tokenta Signal's judgment: the Saturday briefing pointed at one router at one remote facility. The Reuters account points at routers in two states and a flood that reached 17 of 22 en route centers. The story is widening from a single installation mishap toward a system that was supposed to have been replaced in 2006 and is still being maintained for $1 billion a year. L3Harris has moved from silence to cooperation, and Duffy has moved from describing the failure to assigning it. The decisive fact is still missing: installer error or faulty hardware. Until the formal investigation lands, the two-state detail is a lead, not a finding.

WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAINWhat Remains Uncertain

The facts in this update rest on the Reuters report of November 14, 2026, read in full at 21:40 UTC on November 14, 2026, and the USA Today report of the same day, which independently carries the Reuters-sourced findings and the L3Harris statement. The Arkansas and Texas router detail comes from government officials speaking to Reuters and is framed as a possibility, not a confirmed finding; the exact cause of the router failure has not been determined, and no formal root-cause finding has been published.
