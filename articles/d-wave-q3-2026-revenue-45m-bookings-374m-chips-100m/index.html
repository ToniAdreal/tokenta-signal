D-Wave Q3 Revenue Up 21% as Bookings Jump 611% and Backlog Hits $38.3 Million

D-Wave Quantum reported third-quarter 2026 revenue of $4.5 million, up 21% year over year and ahead of the $4.33 million IBES estimate. Nine-month bookings reached $37.4 million, up 611%, and remaining performance obligations totaled $38.3 million, up 1,221%, with about 59% expected to convert within 12 months. WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

D-Wave Quantum Inc. (NASDAQ: QBTS) reported third-quarter 2026 revenue of $4.5 million, up 21% year over year, beating the $4.33 million IBES estimate. Professional services revenue rose $1.5 million and quantum-computing-as-a-service revenue rose $0.8 million, partly offset by a $1.5 million drop in system sales. Third-quarter bookings fell 19% year over year to $1.9 million, while bookings for the first nine months reached $37.4 million, up 611%.

The backlog is the headline number. Remaining performance obligations totaled $38.3 million at September 30, up 1,221% from $2.9 million a year earlier, with about 59% expected to convert to revenue within 12 months and about 71% within 24 months. Management said most annual revenue would be recognized in the fourth quarter, with deliveries of annealing systems to two key customers.

The loss narrowed on paper but the cost base widened. Net loss was $48.2 million, or $0.13 per share, down $91.8 million year over year, almost entirely because non-cash warrant-liability charges fell $121.9 million. GAAP operating expenses rose 84% to $55.9 million, adjusted EBITDA loss widened 83% to $37.7 million, and GAAP gross margin fell to 52.9% from 71.4% on a heavier mix of lower-margin services work. Cash and marketable securities stood at $503.4 million.

WHY IT MATTERSThe order book is growing 30 times faster than the revenue line

D-Wave's revenue is still tiny at $4.5 million a quarter, so the quarter itself says little about the business. The number that matters is the $38.3 million of contracted work waiting to be recognized, up from $2.9 million a year ago, with nine-month bookings of $37.4 million behind it. That backlog, if it converts on schedule, is worth roughly two years of current quarterly revenue.

The conversion test comes fast. About 59% of the backlog is expected to become revenue within 12 months, and management pointed to fourth-quarter system deliveries to two key customers. A quantum vendor's backlog is only as good as its delivery record, so the next two quarters will show whether the 1,221% jump is a pipeline or a press release.

THE HONEST CHECKWashington money and a second production deployment

D-Wave signed a definitive agreement with the U.S. Department of Commerce for access to up to $100 million in CHIPS and Science Act funding, paid in installments against milestones, to support a 100,000-qubit annealing system and a 10,000-qubit gate-model system targeting 100 logical qubits. The money is contingent, not banked, and the systems are development targets, not shipped products.

The more concrete evidence is customer use. NTT DOCOMO deployed a second production application on D-Wave's hybrid system, cutting daily peak location-registration signals by 65.3% and paging signals by 7.0% across a 333-base-station problem solved in about five minutes. D-Wave also delivered Italy's first annealing quantum computer, a 4,400-plus-qubit Advantage2 system, and opened a gate-model simulator beta with BBVA, FirstQFM, Florida Atlantic University and the Julich Supercomputing Centre. Production deployments and a federal funding line are the two legs the commercialization case stands on; the cost line, up 84% in a quarter, is the weight on the other side.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISA backlog story, not yet a revenue story

Tokenta Signal's judgment: The quarter to watch is the backlog, not the $4.5 million of revenue. A 1,221% jump in remaining performance obligations, a 611% rise in nine-month bookings, a second production deployment at NTT DOCOMO and milestone-contingent federal funding are the strongest commercial signals D-Wave has put up. The honest check is conversion and cost: the backlog has to turn into recognized revenue starting in the fourth quarter, and it has to do so while operating expenses are running 84% higher than a year ago against a 52.9% gross margin. A quantum vendor that books far more than it bills is either about to inflect or about to disappoint, and the next two prints will say which.
