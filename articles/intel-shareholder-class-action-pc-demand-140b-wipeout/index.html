Intel Sued by Shareholders Over PC Demand Disclosure After $140B Rout

Intel faces a proposed shareholder class action filed November 13, 2026 in San Jose federal court, alleging it concealed weakening PC demand and rising inventories before shares fell 28.6% on October 30, wiping out roughly $140 billion in market value. Led by a Michigan pension fund, the suit names CEO Lip-Bu Tan and CFO David Zinsner and points to a $23 billion August stock sale at $95 per share. The allegations are unproven.

WHAT HAPPENEDWhat Happened

Intel faces a proposed shareholder class action alleging it concealed weakening personal-computer demand and rising inventories before its shares fell 28.6% in a single day, wiping out roughly $140 billion in market value. The suit was filed Friday, November 13, 2026, in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California in San Jose, case No. 5:26-cv-13758, and is led by the City of Southfield Fire and Police Retirement System, a Michigan pension fund. Intel, chief executive Lip-Bu Tan and chief financial officer David Zinsner are named as defendants.

The complaint, filed by Robbins Geller Rudman & Dowd, covers investors who bought shares between July 23 and October 29, 2026. It alleges Intel's statements in that window suggested PC demand was improving while failing to disclose weakening demand, rising inventories and their expected effect on revenue, calling the statements materially false and misleading. The plaintiffs say Intel was too slow to disclose how the prolonged PC slowdown and an inventory glut at distributors and manufacturers would weigh on revenue, and that those problems outweighed the benefits of the AI data-center boom.

The suit adds a motive allegation: in mid-August, halfway through what the complaint calls a disastrous third quarter, Intel sold about 242 million shares at $95 apiece, raising $23 billion to fund its chip contract-manufacturing expansion, a key part of Tan's strategy. The crash came on October 30, a day after Intel reported third-quarter revenue of $16.5 billion, up 21% year over year, but guided fourth-quarter revenue to $15 billion to $16 billion, below expectations, and warned weak PC demand could last into the first half of 2027. Shares fell from $92.07 to $65.76 on volume above 455 million shares, and closed Friday, November 13, at $59.66.

WHY IT MATTERSThe case is about timing, not the downturn

The PC weakness itself is not in dispute: Intel disclosed it on October 29, when Client Computing revenue fell 5% to $8.1 billion even as Data Center and AI revenue jumped 78% to $7.3 billion. The legal question is what Intel said between July 23 and October 29, while it was raising $23 billion, and whether its public story matched what its executives knew about the PC business. A company that was optimistic and wrong is not automatically liable; the plaintiffs must show the statements were misleading when made and that the omission caused the loss.

Tan told analysts he was disappointed by manufacturing supply shortages and an unprecedented memory crunch, adding: "We need to address these challenges with urgency." Higher memory prices have made PCs more expensive and dented demand, while supply constraints limited how much Intel could gain from data-center demand. TweakTown notes that under Craig Barrett in the early 2000s, Intel routinely issued mid-quarter updates, and used one in September 2004 to cut its outlook over weaker demand and customer inventory adjustments, much like this quarter. Intel dropped the practice in January 2006.

WHAT TO WATCHWhat to watch next

Intel has not commented on the suit. The next steps are procedural: other investors have 60 days to seek appointment as lead plaintiff once notice is published, and Intel will likely move to dismiss, arguing its statements were forward-looking, the risks were disclosed, or the plaintiffs have not pleaded facts supporting intent. A dismissal motion can take months, and discovery, if the case survives, can take years. Most securities class actions settle rather than go to trial.

The $140 billion figure is the market-value loss on the day, not the damages the class would seek; those are measured by the difference between the price investors paid and the price the stock would have traded at if the truth had been disclosed, a calculation that requires an event study and expert testimony. Operationally, the watch is the same set of facts the suit is built on: whether PC demand stabilizes in the first half of 2027 as Intel warned it might not, whether memory prices ease, and whether the fourth-quarter numbers land inside the $15 billion to $16 billion guide.

SIGNAL JUDGMENT — ANALYSISA boom on one side, a lawsuit on the other

Tokenta Signal's judgment: this suit is a disclosure case wearing a market-crash costume. Intel's third quarter was genuinely split, Data Center and AI up 78% while the PC business fell 5%, and the market punished the guidance, not the quarter. The plaintiffs' real argument is narrower and harder to dismiss: that between July and October, while Intel sold $23 billion of stock at $95, its public statements described a PC recovery that its own order books contradicted. If the court lets the class proceed, the documents will decide whether that was optimism or omission. Either way, the episode revives an old question Intel answered in 2006 by dropping mid-quarter updates: when demand turns, does silence protect the stock, or does it just defer the reckoning and add a lawsuit to it?
